An interactive companion to the sky-color reconstruction. Scrub the timeline to watch the atmosphere change from the steam-and-CO₂ Hadean to today, then pick an epoch and scrub through a day to see how its sky moved from dawn to dusk. Every color comes from the same spectral radiative-transfer model as the report.

A day under that sky

A whole-sky (fisheye) view: the zenith is at the center and the horizon is the rim, north at the top. Equinox geometry, so the Sun rises due east at 6:00 and sets due west at 18:00 everywhere; at the poles the noon Sun sits only 15° above the horizon. The Moon is placed for the selected date and clock time at the selected latitude, on your time zone's central meridian. On this dome the Sun and Moon are enlarged together so the phase stays readable; in the VR view both are drawn at four times their true angular size. Moonlight is the same sky grid as sunlight, added on top, with the Moon at its own place in the sky. Its brightness follows the lunar phase and the Moon's angular size at that epoch. When the Moon covers part of the Sun, sunlight throughout the sky is scaled by the fraction of that drawn solar disk still visible, so a partial eclipse is more common than for the true sizes, and a true overlap is still covered. Both disks change size: the Moon with its distance on the date, an Earth radius closer when overhead, and the Sun with the Earth's distance through the year. A Moon near perigee covers the Sun completely; near apogee it leaves a ring. From 2.2 billion years ago back the Moon was close enough that every central eclipse is total. For each new Moon the view stands at the best seat, where parallax brings the Moon closest to the Sun. In totality the sky is lit from beyond the Moon's shadow: this epoch's twilight sky, with a sunset glow all round the horizon, about a thousandth as bright as the day sky. The pearly corona and a thin pink rim appear, with Baily's beads and the diamond ring at the edges of totality: the Moon's limb has mountains and valleys (about three times the real relief, so they show on the enlarged disk), and the last sunlight comes through the valleys. Because the disks are drawn enlarged, every phase lasts about four times as long, so play slows tenfold through totality and the annular ring. The thousand brightest stars are drawn on this dome and in the VR view where they stood in the epoch: their present-day places for most, 1815 for the volcanic year, and for the two supernova epochs the thousand brightest at that time, each moved in a straight line from its Hipparcos parallax, proper motion, and radial velocity (Earth's axis keeps today's tilt and direction, since its precession that long ago cannot be pinned down). For the impact winter, 66 million years ago, the Sun and the catalogue's stars are traced back along orbits in the Galaxy instead, keeping only stars long-lived enough to have been shining. Only six of them were brighter than magnitude 4.6; the stars around the Sun then are mostly ones too far away to be catalogued now, so the rest are stand-ins with today's numbers by brightness and spread from the galactic plane, at random places along it. The Sun was then a quarter of the way round its orbit and 100 pc above the plane, so the brightest part of the Milky Way lay elsewhere along the band, and the band sat a little south. Before that, none of today's neighbours were near the Sun, so every star in the older epochs is a stand-in drawn the same way, a different random sky for each age (the three 2.7 Ga epochs share one). The Sun's place on its orbit, which sets where the Milky Way is brightest and how far the band sits from the middle of the sky, is traced back the same way; beyond about a billion years it is only nominal, since small errors in the Galaxy's rotation and the Sun's own drift across the disk add up. Starlight dims through the air, more toward the horizon: the impact winter's soot takes about two magnitudes off every star, so few show, and the Early Hadean's thick CO₂ hides them all. The five naked-eye planets are placed for the selected date and clock time on today's orbits, in every epoch, as the Moon is, and drawn on the same magnitude scale. The faintest of them is a single pixel, and size grows with brightness so that the hundred brightest keep the sizes they already had. Year 2100 adds the communications satellites and, brighter than those, the Sunrise orbital datacenters, both on the same magnitude scale, and both move as the clock does. At night the sky has its own light: airglow and faint starlight, and the Milky Way with its dust lanes and the Magellanic Clouds. In the modern epochs you stand in a mid-sized city, and its lamps light the sky: orange sodium glow in 1980–2000, whiter today and in 2100, brighter still in the polluted city, and almost nothing from the oil lamps of 1815. City skies hide the Milky Way and all but the brighter stars. Each star shows only if it is brighter than the faintest the eye can see against the sky around it, so stars come out one by one in twilight. Night skies are drawn as a dark-adapted eye sees them, so they are brighter than the daytime display curve would give.

Sky along the Sun's vertical: from the horizon under the Sun, up through the zenith, down to the opposite horizon.

Model and data: spherical-shell single scattering with a delta-Eddington multiple-scattering correction, 380–780 nm, CIE 1931 color matching, sRGB output without chromatic adaptation. Colors are what a daylight-balanced camera would record, not what an adapted eye would perceive. Clouds are omitted; paleoatmosphere compositions carry order-of-magnitude uncertainty. Time of day is interpolated between 34 computed solar zenith angles. From the horizon to 20° below it, those samples are spaced 1° apart; from 20° to 30° below, that last sky fades to black. The plane-parallel multiple-scattering term fades out from 10° above the horizon through sunrise. The Moon is a NASA LROC color map (SVS CGI Moon Kit). Moonlight is that same sky grid with the Moon in place of the Sun, added to the sunlight, and scaled by the Allen phase law (Krisciunas & Schaefer 1991) and by the square of the Moon's angular size at that epoch. Its phase is the angle between it and the Sun drawn here. A solar eclipse scales that sunlight by the fraction of the drawn solar disk the Moon leaves uncovered, with the Moon's size from its distance on the date (Schlyter's lunar theory) and the Sun's from the Earth's orbit. In totality the light from outside the shadow is this epoch's sky with the Sun 6° down, averaged round the horizon and scaled to 10−3 of the uneclipsed zenith: measured totality skies are about three orders of magnitude below the day sky, as bright as the end of civil twilight (Sharp, Lloyd & Silverman 1966, Applied Optics 5, 787). Its size follows the Earth–Moon distance at each epoch: cyclostratigraphic distances from Farhat et al. 2022, and about 70% of today's distance at 3.2 Ga from the Moodies Group (Eulenfeld & Heubeck 2023). Ages older than 3.2 Ga extend that trend and stay beyond 30 Earth radii. The thousand brightest stars are Yale Bright Star Catalogue places (Hoffleit & Warren 1991), carried from J2000 to the year on the clock by precession and proper motion. The faintest is a single pixel, and size grows with brightness so that the hundred brightest keep the sizes they already had. The night sky without the Sun or Moon is calibrated in cd/m² (the model's clear zenith with the Sun 45° up set to 2.7 kcd/m², which puts the full-Moon sky near the measured 18 mag/arcsec²). The natural sky is 22.0 mag/arcsec² at the zenith (Leinert et al. 1998), brightening toward the horizon as airglow does. The Milky Way is a model in galactic coordinates (disk, bulge, star clouds, dust including the Great Rift and the Coalsack, the Magellanic Clouds), peaking at 20.2 mag/arcsec² in the Sagittarius star cloud. Each epoch's extinction is about 0.15 mag per airmass from the gas plus 1.086 times its haze or aerosol optical depth; in the impact winter the soot also absorbs the airglow above it. The 66 Ma orbits use a flat rotation curve of 233 km/s, a vertical oscillation of 84 Myr, and the Sun's motion from Schönrich, Binney & Dehnen (2010). City skyglow at the zenith is 18.8 mag/arcsec² today, typical of a mid-sized city centre in the World Atlas (Falchi et al. 2016); about six times the natural sky for 1980–2000 (Cinzano, Falchi & Elvidge 2001); 1.6 times today's in polluted air; today's level in 2100, since the recent brightening of 9.6% a year (Kyba et al. 2023) cannot be projected that far; and a few thousandths of the natural sky in 1815, when light use per head was about 1/6500 of 2000's (Fouquet & Pearson 2006). Clouds over the city are lit from below to six times the clear-sky glow (Kyba et al. 2011). Stars fade out below the naked-eye limiting magnitude for the local sky brightness (the usual conversion from mag/arcsec², 7.93 − 5 log₁₀(10^(4.316 − m/5) + 1)), and twilight fades by about a magnitude per degree of solar depression past 9°. In 2100 the moving points are two populations on that same magnitude scale, kept only while sunlit and brighter than magnitude 6.5. One is the Lawler, Boley, and Rein (2022) megaconstellation, about 65,000 satellites on the filed orbits, each a diffuse sphere of effective area 0.8 m². The other is Blue Origin's Sunrise orbital datacenters from Boley, Lawler, and Rein (2026): 51,600 Sun-synchronous satellites between 500 and 1,800 km, nodes spread 10° to either side of the terminator, each a diffuse sphere of 800 m² at albedo 0.2.
