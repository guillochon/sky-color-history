\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

What did the sky look like from the ground in the Archean, under the young Sun, with the Moon nearer? We reconstruct the whole view from Earth's surface through its history, at twenty-three moments and the years between them, from a proto-Earth 17 million years after the first solids to the year 2100: the color of the sky by day and at dusk, computed with a spectral radiative-transfer model of each epoch's air, and on that sky everything else that could be seen from the ground: the Sun and its spots, the Moon's size, face and eclipses, the planets and their moons, the stars and the Galaxy as they then lay, meteors, comets, aurorae, airglow, halos, clouds and rainbows, and in the latest epochs the light people added. It began as a study of the sky's color, which remains its quantitative core, and grew with the interactive site built on the model, which draws each of these and is the source of every number quoted here outside the color tables.

The epochs (Table~\ref{tab:epochlist}) were chosen for what changed: the air (a 30-bar CO$_2$ Hadean, an Archean with no oxygen and its episodes of organic haze, the first ozone after the Great Oxidation, the Snowball glaciations, the oxygen-rich Carboniferous, the impact winter after Chicxulub, a volcanic year, the ozone hole and today's cities), the sky above it (a Moon forming and receding, a young and active Sun, a Solar System before the giant planets' instability, nearby supernovae, stars that outshone Sirius, a bombardment and a possible ring), and the view people have made (city light and a sky full of satellites). Each is a reconstruction, with the uncertainties the sections state: some quantities are measured, some modeled, some estimated to show a trend, and some, like where the planets stood on a given day four billion years ago, unknowable and drawn at random. The epochs are fixed points on one timeline: between them the site runs the air, the Sun, the Moon, the planets and the stars on through every year, and some epochs are events with dates and durations of their own, the haze episodes, the glaciations, the impact winter, the volcanic year (Section~\ref{method-time}). The sky's own dated events come on their days too: the supernovae, novae and stellar eruptions that outshone third magnitude, the real comets, volcanic veils, great aurorae, meteor storms and impacts, and four events still to come (Section~\ref{events}).

This is a living document. Much of what it draws rests on questions that are still argued: when the giant planets went unstable and whether a fifth giant was lost; how far away the Moon was in its first hundred million years; how thick the Archean haze was and how much ozone the first oxygen made; how old Saturn's rings are; whether the Earth had a ring in the Ordovician; how bright the young Sun's corona and wind were. Where the literature disagrees, we say which reading we take and why, and as the consensus moves the site and this report will move with it: epochs will be redrawn, numbers revised and new findings added. Each version carries its date, and the code and data that produce it are public, so any figure here can be traced to the model and the sources behind it.

The site computes what it shows as realistically as it can while still loading in seconds and running smoothly in a browser, and where the two conflict it compromises. Some compromises stand in for physics too costly to run. Jupiter's and Saturn's clouds are a tracer stirred by a flow made of noise, shaped by the measured winds and rolled into eddies and billows, a proxy for the planets' true three-dimensional hydrodynamics rather than a simulation of it; the animation repeats over a 2000-day cycle. The sky's multiple scattering is solved ahead of time on a grid of Sun heights and interpolated between them, with the orders past the fourth spread evenly in all directions rather than followed. The clouds are scenery, procedural volumes lit by the model's sky rather than the output of a climate model, and the Milky Way is a model of its structure rather than a photograph. The photosphere's granulation and the Moon's craters' shadows are drawn from procedural cells and a limited march toward the Sun, and the close-up detail of Jupiter, Saturn, the Sun and the spacecraft maps is built only when the view zooms in on them. Other compromises are made for the eye: the Sun and Moon are drawn at four times their size, the sunset's colors are spread further apart than they are, and the Sun's limb darkening is softened. Each section says where its subject is measured, modeled or approximated.

In Section~\ref{method} we describe how the view is reconstructed, at the epochs and between them. Section~\ref{sky} gives the sky's colors by day and at dusk, and Section~\ref{sun} through Section~\ref{people} take in turn the Sun, the Moon, the planets, the stars and the Galaxy, the night and what crosses it, and the sky people made. Section~\ref{epochs} goes through the epochs one by one, and Section~\ref{caveats} says what the reconstruction leaves out and which of its numbers are estimates.

\subsection{Has this been done before?}\label{done-before}

Not as such. A search of the Valency corpus (arXiv, PubMed, EarthArXiv and others) finds the two halves of the sky-color problem solved separately but never joined. On one side, atmospheric-optics work computes modern sky color rigorously: \protect\hyperlink{cite.lynch2005}{Lynch \& Mazuk (2005)} derive CIE chromaticities of the sky, Sun and distant objects from MODTRAN spectra; \protect\hyperlink{cite.peyvandi2016}{Peyvandi et al. (2016)} simulate 5.6 million daylight spectra with SBDART to map how aerosol and ozone shift sky color; \protect\hyperlink{cite.lee2017}{Lee \& Mollner (2017)} show with Monte Carlo simulation that vivid solar-horizon twilight colors need aerosol while the purest antisolar colors occur in a purely molecular atmosphere. On the other side, paleoatmosphere work constrains composition: \protect\hyperlink{cite.catling2020}{Catling \& Zahnle (2020)} for the Archean (O$_2$ below 10$^{-6}$ of present, CO$_2$ 10--2,500$\times$ modern, CH$_4$ 10$^{2}$--10$^{4}\times$ modern), and \protect\hyperlink{cite.arney2016}{Arney et al. (2016)}, \protect\hyperlink{cite.mak2023}{Mak et al. (2023)} and \protect\hyperlink{cite.goldblatt2024}{Goldblatt et al. (2024)} for the organic-haze episodes. Arney's ``Pale Orange Dot'' computes what a hazy Archean Earth looks like \emph{from space}, as an exoplanet spectrum, and \protect\hyperlink{cite.sanroma2012}{Sanromá \& Pallé (2012)} Earth's light curves over the last 500 million years; that is as close as the literature comes. Nobody has computed the view from the ground, or the horizon-versus-zenith, latitude, and sunset structure.

The same holds for the rest of the view. Its pieces are separate results: the Moon's recession and the day's lengthening (\protect\hyperlink{cite.farhat2022}{Farhat et al. 2022}), the young Sun's brightness and activity, the stars' past motions, the giant planets' early instability. Earth's transmission spectrum has been computed through geological time as a transiting exoplanet would show it (\protect\hyperlink{cite.kaltenegger2020}{Kaltenegger, Lin \& Madden 2020}), and today's lunar eclipse has been modeled with refraction (\protect\hyperlink{cite.garciamunoz2012}{García Muñoz et al. 2012}), but no reconstruction of how the eclipsed Moon itself looked in past epochs turns up, nor of how these pieces together looked from the ground on a given evening. We assemble them here.

\section{How the view is reconstructed}\label{method}


\subsection{The sky's colors}\label{method-colors}

For each epoch the atmosphere is built from components with their own vertical profile and spectral optical depth: Rayleigh-scattering gas (N$_2$, O$_2$, CO$_2$, CH$_4$, H$_2$O in their epoch-appropriate partial pressures), ozone (the Chappuis cross section of \protect\hyperlink{cite.serdyuchenko2014}{Serdyuchenko et al. 2014} at 223 K, on a layer whose peak falls from 26 km at the equator to 18 km at the pole), tropospheric aerosol, a stratospheric sulfate layer, a tholin-like fractal organic haze (strongly blue-absorbing, forward-scattering), and impact soot (Table~\ref{tab:epochs}). Sky radiance is integrated numerically along each line of sight through a spherical-shell atmosphere, so grazing sunlight at twilight is handled properly. The light scattered more than once is carried by a table over altitude and the Sun's local elevation, as in \protect\hyperlink{cite.hillaire2020}{Hillaire (2020)}: from each of its nodes, rays in 576 directions gather the light scattered once on its way there and the sunlight off the ground; every later order is then solved exactly, as a linear system for each wavelength, with the ground lit by the scattered light too, and the first four orders keep their directions, which the forward-scattering hazes and aerosols need. Against CalcMySky (\protect\hyperlink{cite.kabatsayev2025}{Kabatsayev 2025}), an independent multiple-scattering code after \protect\hyperlink{cite.bruneton2008}{Bruneton \& Neyret (2008)} that Stellarium uses (\protect\hyperlink{cite.zotti2023}{Zotti \& Wolf 2023}), run on the same atmospheres, the modern sky agrees within 2\% at the zenith from noon until the Sun is 18$^\circ$ below the horizon and within 0.002 in chromaticity, the hazy Archean and impact skies within 12\% by day and 20\% in twilight, and the 30-bar Hadean within 20\% at the zenith wherever CalcMySky itself converges (600--750 nm). Spectra from 380--780 nm are converted to CIE XYZ with the analytic color-matching functions of \protect\hyperlink{cite.wyman2013}{Wyman, Sloan \& Shirley (2013)} and shown as sRGB. The model reproduces measured modern values (clear zenith 10,000--20,000 K, horizon 6,000--8,500 K, setting Sun $\sim$1,700 K, pink-lavender antisolar Belt of Venus) before being pointed backward.

We treat the young Sun as a blackbody whose effective temperature and luminosity follow the solar track of \protect\hyperlink{cite.baraffe2015}{Baraffe et al. (2015)}: 5,530 K and 68\% of today's output at 4.5 Ga, rising through 5,620 K (75\%) at 3.8 Ga, 5,660 K (80\%) at 2.7 Ga and 5,700 K (85\%) at 2.2 Ga to 5,772 K today, and 4,614 K (47\%) for the 17-million-year-old Sun of 4.55 Ga; between the epochs the temperature runs linearly in time and the inverse of the luminosity too, the form of the brightening that \protect\hyperlink{cite.gough1981}{Gough (1981)} gives. The color effect is small: the 5,530 K Sun of 4.5 Ga is only about 240 K cooler in color temperature than the modern one, a difference that is barely perceptible side by side and is swamped by atmospheric effects ten to a hundred times larger. The brightness effect is not small, and is why the early-epoch skies are rendered dimmer. We ignore Fraunhofer lines and the young Sun's ultraviolet excess, which matter for haze photochemistry but not for visible color. The model is provided as the Python source \emph{skymodel.py}; \emph{run\_epochs.py} produces the sky colors and \emph{limb\_grid.py} the limb and disk colors.

\subsection{Between the epochs}\label{method-time}

The epochs are fixed points rather than the only times the view can show. The site's timeline runs from the Solar System's birth to 250,000 years ahead, and each epoch is exact at its own year, while between the epochs every quantity runs along a curve suited to it. The air (\emph{airtime.py}) is an epoch's own at that epoch's year. Between epochs its gases run log-linearly in time from one to the next, since drawdowns and rises run roughly exponentially, with the steps the geological record shows added as fixed points of their own: the Great Oxidation Event's rise of O$_2$ and fall of CH$_4$ between 2.43 Ga (\protect\hyperlink{cite.gumsley2017}{Gumsley et al. 2017}) and 2.22 Ga (\protect\hyperlink{cite.poulton2021}{Poulton et al. 2021}), O$_2$ held near 1\% of today's through the mid-Proterozoic until the Neoproterozoic rise from about 800 Ma (\protect\hyperlink{cite.lyons2014}{Lyons, Reinhard \& Planavsky 2014}), and the Cambrian's 5--10\% O$_2$ (\protect\hyperlink{cite.mills2023}{Mills et al. 2023}). Ozone is not interpolated on its own but follows the O$_2$, by the global-mean columns \protect\hyperlink{cite.cooke2021}{Cooke et al. (2022)} simulate at fixed oxygen levels (18 DU at 0.1\% of today's, 66 at 1\%, 136 at 5\%, 169 at 10\%, 279 at today's), scaled at each epoch to that epoch's own columns by latitude. The tropospheric aerosol's optical depth runs log-linearly between the epochs, the Sun as above, the meteoric dust of the Ordovician break-up with the dust's own rise and decay (\emph{Dust, comets and the Ordovician ring}), and the Moon-forming impact, about 4.51 Ga, is a step: the proto-Earth's air before it and the early Hadean's from it on. Figure~\ref{fig:airtime} shows the result.

Some epochs are events that interrupt a longer-lived sky, and the timeline gives them dates and durations. The Neoarchean's haze episodes interrupt a clear background, which isotope records from South Africa and Western Australia keep returning to (\protect\hyperlink{cite.zerkle2012}{Zerkle et al. 2012}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.izon2015}{Izon et al. 2015}). Three episodes are recorded in the 2.65--2.5 Ga Ghaap Group alone (\protect\hyperlink{cite.zerkle2012}{Zerkle et al. 2012}); we date them about 2.63, 2.58 and 2.52 Ga by spacing the drill core's ends evenly, so to within tens of millions of years. The youngest, which \protect\hyperlink{cite.izon2017}{Izon et al. (2017)} resolved, set in over 0.3 Myr and lasted 1.4 Myr in all, and each episode is given that shape: the two older are the thin haze and the youngest the thick. Snowball Earth is two glaciations over a non-glacial Cryogenian: the Sturtian from about 717 Ma (\protect\hyperlink{cite.maclennan2018}{MacLennan et al. 2018}) to 660.98 Ma (\protect\hyperlink{cite.rooney2020}{Rooney et al. 2020}), and the Marinoan, about four million years long (\protect\hyperlink{cite.tasistrohart2025}{Tasistro-Hart et al. 2025}), ending at 635.21 Ma (\protect\hyperlink{cite.prave2016}{Prave et al. 2016}). Once the ice passed a critical latitude it reached the equator within years to decades (\protect\hyperlink{cite.eberhard2023}{Eberhard et al. 2023}), and it melted in about two thousand years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hoffman2017}{Hoffman et al. 2017}). The K--Pg impact winter lets almost no sunlight through for over a year, and its soot has cleared about five years after the impact (\protect\hyperlink{cite.bardeen2017}{Bardeen et al. 2017}): the site holds it for a year and clears it with a one-year e-folding time, so its sky shows for about two years over a Late Cretaceous of today's air. Tambora's sulfate veil spreads through the stratosphere over the months after April 1815, stays through 1816 and clears in 1817, and the ozone-hole sky holds from 1980 to 2000. Three more volcanic veils come over the modern-era skies as events without epochs of their own, each Tambora's air at its own veil's depth: Laki's sulfur haze from June 1783, over the Northern Hemisphere only and at Tambora's depth, though most of it lay in the troposphere (\protect\hyperlink{cite.thordarson2003}{Thordarson \& Self 2003}), and Krakatoa's of 1883 and Pinatubo's of 1991, about 0.14 and 0.15 at 550 nm at their peaks (\protect\hyperlink{cite.sato1993}{Sato et al. 1993}), each fading with an e-folding time of about a year. As an event comes on, its own parts of the air (the haze and methane, the snowball's ice and clean air, the soot and sulfate, the ozone column) go from the background's to its own, while the rest stays the background's.

The sky's colors cannot simply be blended between epochs as different as these, so the parts of the sky that are computed ahead of time (the day-cycle colors, the setting Sun's bands, the crepuscular rays' tables, the light in the Earth's shadow and the spectra) are computed for 143 airs along the timeline (\emph{airgrid.py}). They are every epoch's own, the years where the curves bend, and more wherever blending the two neighbors would miss the air between them, at any of a set of test directions and Sun heights, by more than 0.02 in log$_{\rm 10}$ luminance (about 5\%) or 0.002 in chromaticity, found by halving each interval in turn. Each event is computed at steps of its strength in the same way. The site blends the two airs either side of the time shown, luminance in logarithm and chromaticity linearly, by the year or by the event's strength. The 62 background airs lie densest where the air changes fastest: through the Hadean's CO$_2$ drawdown, the Great Oxidation and the Ordovician dust. The starlight's extinction, the soot's absorption, the airglow and the cloud cover follow the same blended air, and everything else follows the year too: the Sun's brightness and spots, the Moon's distance, orbit and face and the day's length, the planets' moons and rings, the stars, the Sun's place in the Galaxy, the rates of comets, meteors and dust, and the glow of cities, as the sections that follow describe.

\subsection{The rest of the view}\label{method-view}

The sky's colors come from the radiative-transfer model described above; the rest of the view is drawn on them by the interactive site, in two views: a dome, the whole sky as a fisheye, and a first-person view in WebGL, with a walkable ground and clouds. Both draw on one photometric scale, calibrated in absolute units: the clear zenith with the Sun 45$^\circ$ up is 2.7 kcd/m$^{2}$, which puts the full-Moon sky at the measured 18 mag/arcsec$^{2}$. Stars, planets, meteors and satellites are placed by their magnitudes on that scale and dimmed by the air, 1.086 magnitudes per airmass for each unit of its optical depth at 550 nm, about 0.15 of it from the gas and the rest from the aerosol, haze, sulfate and soot; each shows only when it is brighter than the naked-eye limit for the sky around it. The Moon, moonlight, supernovae, fireballs and city glow light the sky through the same model. The clock runs over one solar day of the time shown, and the Sun follows the seasons for the date, with today's 23.4$^\circ$ tilt at each epoch's own year and the long-term precession around it, since the Moon has held the obliquity between about 22$^\circ$ and 24.5$^\circ$. The Sun and Moon are drawn at four times their angular size so that their disks show on a screen, and zooming in acts as a telescope, down to a field of 14 arcseconds.

Where each body was is carried back as far as it can be known, for any year. The Moon's distance follows the tidal record, the Sun's brightness and size its evolutionary track, and the stars their measured motions over the last two million years; beyond that the star field, and the planets' positions more than 10,000 years from today, are drawn at random with the right statistics, since no calculation can recover them. The colors are shown as a daylight-balanced camera would record them, without chromatic adaptation, and the night as a dark-adapted eye sees it. The scenery and the clouds are drawn, not modeled; the sky is the model.


\subsection{The ground}\label{ground}

The ground is scenery, like the clouds: a walkable landscape chosen for each era rather than computed, lit by the model's sky and Sun. The proto-Earth has magma pools; the 4.5 Ga Earth a hot ocean, about 230 $^\circ$C while 100 to 200 bars of CO$_2$ remained (\protect\hyperlink{cite.zahnle2010}{Zahnle et al. 2010}), steaming round volcanic islands; the Archean, water and volcanic shields; Snowball Earth, ice plateaus with blue banded cliffs and frozen ponds, open water again in the Cryogenian between the glaciations; the Ordovician, shallow seas with no land plants beyond mosses and crusts; the Carboniferous, forest and swamps; the Late Cretaceous, forest, dead woods in its impact winter; the Pleistocene, 1816 and 1900, woods and snow-capped peaks; and the modern epochs, a town with lit windows. The ground's color, the snow on the peaks, the Snowball's snow cover and its ice halos cross-fade with the year and with an event's strength, while the layout of the scenery changes with the epoch. Water reflects the sky and glitters in the Sun and Moon; lava glows; distant scenery fades into the sky's color. None of this is part of the model: the sky above it is.

\subsection{Horizon versus zenith}\label{horizon-versus-zenith}

In a clean molecular atmosphere the zenith is blue and the horizon white because the horizon path is long enough that blue is scattered back out before reaching the eye and multiple scattering fills in the other colors. The size of that gradient is the clearest single diagnostic of atmospheric state. It is largest in the cleanest atmospheres (Snowball Earth, today's clean air: 6,800--11,500 K of color-temperature difference at mid-latitudes and the poles), shrinks as Rayleigh depth rises (late Hadean), collapses to nothing when a broadband absorber or thick scatterer sits overhead (hazy Archean, impact winter, 30-bar Hadean), and falls to a third or less under a stratospheric sulfate veil or heavy pollution, which whiten the zenith toward the horizon's color.


\subsection{Equator, mid-latitudes, poles}\label{equator-mid-latitudes-poles}

Latitude enters through solar elevation and surface albedo. A high Sun lights the sky from above through a short path, giving a brighter but paler zenith (equatorial noon $\sim$8,300 K); a low Sun sends light through more air, so the polar summer zenith is a deeper, darker blue ($\sim$17,800 K today, 16,800 K over Snowball ice) while the polar horizon is warmer and yellower. The same geometry makes the poles the place where any overhead absorber is most visible: the hazy Archean sky is cream at the equator but distinctly orange-yellow at the poles, and the impact-winter Sun that is still a pale disk at the equator is gone at 75$^\circ$ latitude. Snow and ice brighten the dome and deepen the zenith's blue by reflecting light back into the atmosphere, though polar and Snowball horizons still whiten as a land horizon does.


\subsection{Particulates}\label{particulates}

Three kinds of particle appear in the record, and each leaves a different sky. Non-absorbing sulfate high in the stratosphere (volcanic years) whitens the day sky and produces long, luminous pink and lavender twilights, because the layer is still sunlit after the ground is dark. Absorbing organic haze (Neoarchean) or soot (impact winter) removes blue outright, turning the dome tan or amber with almost no directional structure, and dims the surface by factors of 3--20. Boundary-layer pollution or dust brightens and grays the zenith, and buries the setting Sun in murk before it reaches the horizon. Tropospheric aerosol is also what makes sunset color vivid: the model confirms the result of \protect\hyperlink{cite.lee2017}{Lee \& Mollner (2017)} that a purely molecular sky yields a pale, yellow-orange horizon while any turbidity deepens it to red.


\subsection{Sunsets and sunrises through time}\label{sunsets-and-sunrises-through-time}

The biggest surprise is ozone. Today the sky above a setting Sun grades from orange through pale yellow to a zenith that stays blue well into twilight: about 24,000 K with the Sun 4$^\circ$ down. That persistent blue is the Chappuis band, ozone absorbing yellow and orange along the grazing path. The same modern sky with the ozone removed drops that zenith to a neutral gray, about 6,700 K. Before the Great Oxidation Event there was no ozone, so every sunset for the first two billion years ended with the zenith fading to gray. At $\sim$2.2 Ga a 66 DU column, the \protect\hyperlink{cite.cooke2021}{Cooke et al. (2022)} global mean for 1\% of present oxygen, turns that gray zenith a pale blue near 8,400 K. The deep twilight blue arrives later, once the column is near modern: the snowball sky, at their 10\% oxygen column of 169 DU, holds a dusk zenith near 16,000 K. The same thinning is visible in the ozone-hole years. A polar-spring column of 130 DU leaves the dusk zenith pale blue, near 9,900 K, against 24,000 K today. Beyond the ozone story: Hadean sunsets were not events at the horizon but a slow dimming of the whole glowing dome; hazy Archean sunsets were the longest and most saturated in Earth's history, vermilion at the horizon and amber overhead; Snowball sunsets were brief and golden; the sunsets of the impact winter did not exist as color at all; and the reddest, most theatrical skies of the Phanerozoic belong to volcanic years like 1815 and 1883.

\subsection{Clouds}\label{clouds}

Clouds are not part of the radiative-transfer model; the first-person view draws them as scenery, ray-marched through a volume and lit by the model's sky, the Sun and the Moon, and by a supernova, a fireball or city light when there is one. Their regimes are illustrative choices for each era rather than results: a near-overcast stratiform deck under the steamy Hadean CO$_2$ (88\% cover at 4.5 Ga), scattered cumulus in the Archean thinning as the haze thickens (from 48\% at 3.8 Ga to 16\% under the thickest haze), sparse thin cloud over the frozen Snowball (22\%), towering convection in the warm Carboniferous (82\%), almost none in the impact winter (8\%), and more cirrus in the volcanic year. Between the epochs the regime runs from one epoch's to the next's with the year, and toward an event's own with its strength. The weather changes over days and the clouds through each day: cumulus days build to an afternoon peak and stratiform ones burn off by midday. The kind of cloud changes from day to day as well as the amount: ahead of a front the cloud spreads into sheets and the cirrus thickens, behind it the cold air builds cumulus, an unstable day grows taller towers than a stable one, and the base sits lower on a damp day than a dry one, each about the era's own regime. Each date has its own clouds, and the noise they are drawn from is read on a slanted slice so its 9 km tile does not repeat across the sky. After sunset the clouds stay lit from below until the Earth's shadow climbs past them.

\subsection{Crepuscular rays}\label{crepuscular-rays}

Where the Sun shines from behind a cloud, beams fan out across the sky and the sky between them darkens: crepuscular rays, the cloud's shadow in the sunlit air. Only the part of each direction's light that is sunlight scattered once in the lower air can be shadowed, so the page computes that share from each air along the timeline, its gas, aerosol, dust, haze, sulfate and soot layers with their heights, for every Sun height and direction (\emph{shafts.py}); along each line of sight the stretches whose path to the Sun passes through cloud lose their share. The rays are strongest where the scattering sits low, and weakest in the 30-bar Hadean, whose light is multiply scattered and comes from everywhere at once. The Moon makes the same rays through gaps in cloud, and so does a bright fireball.


\subsection{Rainbows}\label{rainbows}

Rain falls from the clouds drawn above it, from the same day-to-day weather: steady rain under the thicker parts of a deck when a front comes, showers under the tallest cells on an unstable afternoon, drizzle from a low damp deck, and snow when the ground is near freezing or below. Where sunlight reaches the falling rain through gaps in the cloud, the drops scatter it as water spheres do, so a shower with the Sun behind the eye shows its rainbows: the primary 42$^\circ$ from the point opposite the Sun, red on the outside, with the sky inside it brighter; Alexander's dark band beyond it; and the fainter secondary at 51$^\circ$, its colors reversed. The drops' scattering is worked out by geometric optics for each number of reflections inside the drop, with the Fresnel share of each polarization, and by Airy theory about the primary and secondary bows (\protect\hyperlink{cite.airy1838}{Airy 1838}), which sets their widths and the supernumerary bands inside them. It is spread over a range of drop sizes and blurred by the Sun's disk, and colored through the solar spectrum; against Lorenz--Mie theory for drops of 0.1 and 0.3 mm the primary's peak falls within 0.05$^\circ$ and its height within 4\%, while the secondary comes out 15--30\% faint. The drop size comes from what is falling: steady rain and showers by the Marshall--Palmer median drop (\protect\hyperlink{cite.marshall1948}{Marshall \& Palmer 1948}), up to 1 mm, past which drops flatten and their bows break up; drizzle, of 0.15 mm, gives a broad, pale bow. Snow makes none.

The bows move with the epoch, because their radii depend on how much more strongly water bends light than the air around it. Water's refractive index is taken at the rain's temperature (\protect\hyperlink{cite.harvey1998}{Harvey et al. 1998}), and the air's from each epoch's gases at their pressures. Hot water bends light less, and so does dense air. In the Early Hadean, under 30 bars of CO$_2$ with rain near 85 $^\circ$C, the primary widens to 45.6$^\circ$ and the secondary narrows to 44.6$^\circ$, so the two cross and the dark band between them is gone. In the proto-Earth's guessed 4 bars at 90 $^\circ$C the band narrows from today's 9.7$^\circ$ to 3.7$^\circ$, and in the Late Hadean the primary sits at 42.2$^\circ$. In every later epoch the bows lie within 0.3$^\circ$ of today's 41.8$^\circ$ and 51.5$^\circ$. The Hadean's bows would rarely be seen: in that heat nearly all the rain evaporates before it lands, and little direct sunlight gets through the air. The bow's brightness rests on how much sunlight the drawn clouds let under them, so it is an estimate; the Moon's faint bow is not drawn.


\subsection{Ice halos}\label{ice-halos}

In clear, very cold air the sky fills with diamond dust, tiny hexagonal plates and columns of ice drifting near the ground. Snowball Earth's air would have been full of them at every latitude (Figure~\ref{fig:halos}). Today they are common on the Antarctic plateau and in Arctic winter, so they are drawn at 75$^\circ$ in the modern epochs and in the other cold epochs (2.2 Ga at the end of the Huronian glaciations, the cold Cryogenian between the snowballs, the Late Ordovician and Late Paleozoic ice ages, and the Pleistocene), and faintly at 45$^\circ$ at 342 ka, a glacial maximum. Between the epochs their strength runs on with the year, and the Snowball's come and go with its ice.

The halos are traced from the crystals' geometry with the refractive index of ice at 450, 550 and 650 nm (\protect\hyperlink{cite.warren2008}{Warren \& Brandt 2008}), so each arc carries its own dispersion, red toward the Sun: the 22$^\circ$ and 46$^\circ$ halos, the upper and lower tangent arcs, the sun dogs (which move out with the Sun's height and vanish above 61$^\circ$), the circumzenithal arc (only while the Sun is below 32$^\circ$), the parhelic circle with its 120$^\circ$ parhelia, a sun pillar for a low Sun, and the diffraction aureole. The Moon makes the same, faintly. Their brightness, set against the clean noon sky, is an estimate of a good display; the air in front of them is each epoch's.

\section{The Sun}\label{sun}


\subsection{The young Sun}\label{the-young-sun}

The Sun was about 30\% fainter when the Earth formed and has brightened since, as hydrogen burning has raised the density and temperature of its core. We follow the 1 M$\odot$ track of \protect\hyperlink{cite.baraffe2015}{Baraffe et al. (2015)}: 68\% of today's light and 5,530 K at 4.5 Ga, 80\% and 5,660 K at 2.7 Ga, 94\% at 700 Ma, today's 5,772 K by the Cenozoic, and between the epochs as \protect\hyperlink{cite.gough1981}{Gough (1981)} gives the brightening, the inverse of the luminosity linear in time. Its radius follows from the luminosity and temperature: 0.90 of today's at 4.5 Ga, 0.93 at 2.7 Ga, 0.98 at 700 Ma, so the young Sun's disk was smaller as well as dimmer, and the closer Moon covered it easily. The 4.55 Ga Sun is different. Seventeen million years old, it is still contracting onto the main sequence: cooler, 4,614 K, at 47\% of today's light but 7\% larger than today's, an orange-white disk. The color change is small, two or three hundred kelvin for most of the record, and swamped by the air's; the brightness change is not, and it is why the early skies are drawn dimmer. The Earth--Sun distance varies through the year with today's eccentricity, so the Sun is 3.4\% brighter at perihelion in January.


\subsection{Sunspots}\label{sunspots}

The Sun's disk carries its spots and faculae, drawn from a map of the whole photosphere made for each epoch and day and turned with the Sun's rotation. How spotted the Sun was follows its age. Young solar analogs are spotted over 1 to 10\% of their surface, the coverage falling roughly as the inverse square root of age (\protect\hyperlink{cite.morris2020}{Morris 2020}) and much faster once the rotation period passes about 15 days (\protect\hyperlink{cite.nicholsfleming2020}{Nichols-Fleming \& Blackman 2020}). Stars more active than Rossby number one, the Sun before about 2.5 Gyr, dim when they are active: dark spots dominate their variability. Older, quieter stars like today's Sun brighten with activity, dominated by bright faculae (\protect\hyperlink{cite.shapiro2014}{Shapiro et al. 2014}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.reinhold2019}{Reinhold et al. 2019}), because spot area grows faster with activity than facular area does (\protect\hyperlink{cite.nemec2022}{Nemec et al. 2022}). The Sun's rotation follows the slow-rotator sequence it most likely took (\protect\hyperlink{cite.gallet2013}{Gallet \& Bouvier 2013}): 3.1 days at 4.5 Ga, 16 at 2.7 Ga, 23 at 700 Ma. Between the epochs the spot coverage and spot sizes run geometrically from one epoch's to the next's and the rotation smoothly, by the square of the period.

Fast rotators carry spots at high latitudes and large cool polar caps, as Doppler images of young solar analogs show (\protect\hyperlink{cite.strassmeier2009}{Strassmeier 2009}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.barnes2005}{Barnes et al. 2005}), so the Hadean and early Archean Suns have their spots between 10$^\circ$ and 75$^\circ$ and caps round the poles. A regular cycle sets in at a few hundred million years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.katsova2020}{Katsova 2020}); from 2.7 Ga the spots emerge in a butterfly pattern, from mid-latitudes toward the equator over each cycle (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hathaway2011}{Hathaway 2011}), on cycles lengthening from 8 to 11 years. On Gregorian dates the modern epochs follow the real cycles, each cycle's sunspot number from the SILSO record with the shape of \protect\hyperlink{cite.hathaway1994}{Hathaway, Wilson \& Reichmann (1994)}: the 1816 volcanic year falls in the Dalton Minimum and dates in the Maunder Minimum are all but spotless. Beyond the record a sixth of the time is a grand minimum, as over the Holocene (\protect\hyperlink{cite.usoskin2007}{Usoskin, Solanki \& Kovaltsov 2007}).

Each active region is a bipolar group: a large, round leading spot ahead in the rotation and nearer the equator (Joy's law), a following polarity broken into smaller spots, often with partial penumbrae, and pores between. Peak areas are lognormal (\protect\hyperlink{cite.baumann2005}{Baumann \& Solanki 2005}); a group grows in days and decays linearly, faster the smaller it is, and drifts with the latitude's rotation (\protect\hyperlink{cite.snodgrass1983}{Snodgrass 1983}). The spots have the structure seen in white light (\protect\hyperlink{cite.solanki2003}{Solanki 2003}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.borrero2011}{Borrero \& Ichimoto 2011}): an umbra of 3,700 to 4,600 K, darker the larger it is, sprinkled with umbral dots and crossed in the larger spots by granular light bridges; a penumbra of radial filaments 150 to 650 K below the photosphere, with bright grains at their inner ends and a ragged, comb-like outer edge; and round the region, faculae that show only toward the limb, cleared from a moat round each mature spot, over the faint network of the supergranulation. Intensities are Planck ratios at the color channels' wavelengths, so spots are redder as well as darker. The disk is drawn as a white-light photograph is shown, with umbrae near-black and penumbrae mid-gray. Zoomed further, past the map's resolution of about 1,000 km, the photosphere's own fine structure takes over (\emph{The Sun close up}).

A spot about an arcminute across, some 500 millionths of the hemisphere, is the smallest the eye can see unaided through a haze or a filter (\protect\hyperlink{cite.vaquero2007}{Vaquero 2007}). Today one is on the disk about one day in 25, mostly near solar maximum; at 2.2 Ga every other day; in the Archean and Hadean always, about 30 at once at 4.5 Ga. Spots then take about 3\% off the Sun's light, and they gather toward the poles, darkening the top and bottom of the disk. The young Sun's shape does not change visibly: rotating in 3 days flattens it by only 0.05\%, and even a 1-day rotation by 0.5\%, 10 arcseconds off the width of a 32-arcminute disk.

\subsection{The Sun close up}\label{the-sun-close-up}

Zoomed in, down to a field of view of 14 arcseconds, the photosphere is drawn as high-resolution solar telescopes show it: granulation, convection cells about 1,300 km across, bright where hot gas rises and dark in the narrow lanes where it sinks, living about eight minutes, a fifth of the larger ones splitting from a dark knot late in life (exploding granules); magnetic bright points a couple of hundred kilometers across in the lanes inside faculae; penumbral filaments a few thousand kilometers long; umbral dots about 1,000 K hotter than the umbra around them; and toward the limb the supergranular network, cells some 30,000 km across outlined by brightenings. Spot and plage edges are ragged down to the pixel. This fine structure is the same in every epoch; only the spots and faculae change with the Sun's age (Figure~\ref{fig:sun}).

In the first-person view a Sun near full daylight is not drawn as a disk at all but as the eye sees it: a blinding white blank tinted the epoch's color, in a bloom that whitens the sky around it. Zooming in, as through a filter, dims it to the real disk, and the glare eases off as the Sun sets or is eclipsed, until the corona and Baily's beads take over.

\subsection{The setting Sun}\label{the-setting-sun}

Near the horizon the Sun is drawn as twenty images, one per 20 nm band from 385 to 765 nm, each lifted by its own refraction (the dispersion of air, \protect\hyperlink{cite.edlen1966}{Edlén 1966}) and dimmed by its own optical depth along the grazing path through the epoch's model atmosphere. At the horizon the green and orange images of today's Sun are only about 6 arcseconds apart, so the colors separate only in the last sliver: the green flash is whichever band survives the extinction last, and the cleaner the air the shorter that wavelength. Along the horizontal path, the model's air still passes a twentieth of its peak sunlight at 550 nm in the clean, dry Snowball epoch, the best flashes in the record, and at 630 nm in today's clean air; under the Neoarchean haze, the impact-winter soot, a volcanic veil or a polluted city nothing shorter than 650 to 730 nm gets through, and there is no flash at all.

How far the air lifts the Sun follows its refractivity, the sum over its gases' partial pressures. Today's horizon refraction of about 34 arcminutes is 1.8 times larger at 4.0 Ga (half a bar of CO$_2$ on a bar of N$_2$), 1.11 times in the oxygen-rich Carboniferous and 0.83 times in Snowball Earth's thinner air, so the Sun sets later or earlier by a minute or two. The 30-bar Hadean air is 47 times as refractive as today's: a horizontal ray bends faster than the ground curves and is trapped (ducting), but its Sun is lost in Rayleigh scattering long before it reaches the horizon, so we hold the drawn bending to three times today's.

Each evening also has its own layering of the low air, set by the date so that a given evening always sets the same way. Thin temperature inversions step the disk's edge, show a strip of it twice (once inverted) where the rays cross, and draw a dark line across it where trapped rays run through the densest air; over warm water the Sun meets its own inverted image in an inferior mirage, the ``omega'' sunset. The cold surface of Snowball Earth makes inversions common and inferior mirages rare, and the warm oceans of the Hadean and Archean the reverse. These mirage statistics are illustrative. The disk is also darker and redder toward its edge (limb darkening, \protect\hyperlink{cite.hestroffer1998}{Hestroffer \& Magnan 1998}): the air only multiplies the Sun's light, so the darkening is the same under every sky.

Two of these effects are drawn larger than life. The disk itself is drawn at four times its angular size, as the Moon is, so that it shows at all on a screen, and the bands' separation by refraction is enlarged with it, four times high in the sky and up to 24 times at the horizon, where a real flash is a sliver of a few arcseconds. And the limb darkening is drawn at a fifth of its true strength: on a disk drawn near white, the real contrast reads as a dark rim. The spectrum readout keeps the full law.


\subsection{Solar eclipses}\label{solar-eclipses}

When the Moon covers part of the Sun, all the sunlit sky dims by the fraction of the disk still showing. In totality the sky is lit from beyond the Moon's shadow, tens to a hundred kilometers away, where the Sun is still partly up: the epoch's own twilight sky with a sunset glow all round the horizon, a thousandth of the day sky, as measured (\protect\hyperlink{cite.sharp1966}{Sharp, Lloyd \& Silverman 1966}). The Hadean and hazy Archean totality skies are therefore that era's dusk, peach or amber rather than the deep blue of a modern eclipse.

The corona follows the Sun's age. A younger Sun spun faster and was far more active: its X-ray output goes as age to the $-$1.5 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.gudel1997}{Güdel, Guinan \& Skinner 1997}), about 530 times today's at 4.5 Ga, and its coronal temperature with it, 7.7 MK against 1.5 MK. Today's corona (\protect\hyperlink{cite.baumbach1937}{Baumbach 1937}: electron-scattered K light near the limb, dust-scattered F light beyond about 2.3 solar radii) is scaled accordingly: a denser, more extended K corona, an F corona that follows the era's zodiacal dust, stronger coronal lines (the green Fe XIV and red Fe X, joined by yellow Ca XV in the hot young corona) and a brighter pink chromosphere. These scalings are estimates. The young Sun was also smaller (0.90 of today's radius at 4.5 Ga) while the Moon was closer, so early eclipses hide the inner corona: at 3.8 Ga the Moon looks 1.5 times the Sun's width, and from 2.2 Ga back every central eclipse is total. Through the 30-bar Hadean air, at 5.8 magnitudes of extinction per airmass, behind a Moon eight times the Sun's width, the corona hardly shows: only its outer part clears the Moon's limb, a faint glow lost in the molten Moon's own aureole. The proto-Earth of 4.55 Ga has no Moon, and so no eclipses; its Sun, 17 million years old, has the saturated corona of a star that young, at about 9 MK.

The eclipse itself is drawn from the Moon's real profile: at second and third contact the last sunlight breaks into Baily's beads where the lunar limb's valleys, from the Laser Altimeter's heights, let the photosphere through, and a diamond ring. Like the disks, the eclipses are enlarged: the Sun and Moon are drawn at four times their size, and eclipses are found and timed with the enlarged disks, so the page's partial phases last longer, and its eclipses happen at more places, than real ones. For each new Moon the page stands where parallax brings the Moon closest to the Sun, the best seat for that eclipse.

\section{The Moon}\label{moon}


\subsection{The Moon's orbit through time}\label{the-moon-s-orbit-through-time}

The Moon raises tides on the Earth, and the tidal bulge, carried ahead of it by the Earth's spin, pulls it forward: the Moon has been receding ever since it formed, today by 3.8 cm a year, while the Earth's day lengthens. Run back, the Moon was closer and bigger, its month shorter, and the day shorter too. We take the distance from \protect\hyperlink{cite.farhat2022}{Farhat et al. (2022)}, whose tidal model fits both the present recession and the geological record of tidal rhythmites, back to 3.2 Ga, where \protect\hyperlink{cite.eulenfeld2023}{Eulenfeld \& Heubeck (2023)} find 70\% of today's distance in the Moodies Group rhythmites; older ages extend the long-term drift. At 4.5 Ga, some ten million years after the giant impact, the Moon is still at about 8 Earth radii: \protect\hyperlink{cite.korenaga2023}{Korenaga (2023)} finds that a Moon whose magma ocean was still solidifying raised little tide in itself and had receded only to 7 to 9 Earth radii by the time the ocean froze. (Table~\ref{tab:moon} gives the numbers by epoch; between the epochs the distance follows the same history, year by year.)

The day follows from the distance by holding the Earth--Moon angular momentum fixed: a closer Moon means a faster-spinning Earth. This reproduces the day lengths \protect\hyperlink{cite.farhat2022}{Farhat et al. (2022)} give for their distances to within a tenth of an hour, 21.9 hours at 700 Ma and 15.6 hours at 2.7 Ga, and at 4.5 Ga it gives 5.9 hours. The year is the same length in seconds in every epoch, since the Earth's orbit is set by the Sun's mass, which has barely changed; so a year held 400 days in Snowball Earth, 562 at 2.7 Ga and 1,486 at 4.5 Ga. The month, by Kepler's third law, was 22.6 of today's days at 2.2 Ga and 15.3 at 4.0 Ga, still 28 to 31 of each epoch's own shorter days; at 2.7 Ga a year held about eighteen months, as tidal rhythmites suggest. At 4.5 Ga the Moon went round in 32 hours, five and a half of that epoch's days. The proto-Earth of 4.55 Ga has no Moon; how fast it turned before the impact cannot be known, and its 12-hour day is a guess.

The orbit's tilt to the ecliptic also changed. We carry today's 5.15$^\circ$ back along these distances with the semi-analytic tidal model of \protect\hyperlink{cite.cuk2016}{Ćuk et al. (2016)}: the Earth's tides tilt the orbit less as the Moon recedes, and tides raised in the Moon itself damp the tilt, most strongly near 33 Earth radii, where the Moon's own obliquity swings large. The orbit comes out a little steeper in the past, 5.5$^\circ$ at 2.7 Ga and 5.8$^\circ$ at 4.0 Ga; Ćuk et al. argue these are lower bounds, and the 17$^\circ$ to 30$^\circ$ and more that such models give for the young Moon belong before 33 Earth radii, older than any epoch here but 4.5 Ga. Inside the Laplace radius the orbit precesses about the Earth's equator rather than the ecliptic (\protect\hyperlink{cite.tremaine2009}{Tremaine, Touma \& Namouni 2009}, eq. 24): 8.4 Earth radii for the present Earth. A fluid Earth spinning in 5.9 hours carries a larger bulge, and the radius moves out by (24/5.9)$^{2/5}$, to about 15 Earth radii, so the 4.5 Ga Moon is drawn in the plane of the equator. The orbit was more eccentric too, growing from today's 0.055 to about 0.1 at 4.0 Ga (\protect\hyperlink{cite.daher2021}{Daher et al. 2021}), after it had been through the evection resonance. In the \protect\hyperlink{cite.cuk2012}{Ćuk \& Stewart (2012)} example that resonance holds the eccentricity near 0.45 from about 9,000 to 70,000 years, and by 4.5 Ga tides have damped the orbit nearly round again. With the eccentricity the Moon rocks east and west each month by about twice the eccentricity in radians, $\pm$6.3$^\circ$ today and $\pm$12$^\circ$ at 4.0 Ga, and north and south by its obliquity, 6.7$^\circ$ today and 21$^\circ$ to 23$^\circ$ at 3.8 and 4.0 Ga, as it nears the Cassini-state transition; the page draws the globe on these axes, so the Moon visibly librates.

The consequences show in the sky. At 3.8 Ga the Moon is one and a half times today's width and more than twice as bright when full. At 4.5 Ga it is 4$^\circ$ across, seven and a half times today's width and 57 times its area, and so close that it is placed as seen from the ground: it shifts 7$^\circ$ against the stars between the horizon and the zenith. It crosses the sky in hours, rising and setting several times a day. In the plane of the equator, it eclipses the Sun at every new Moon for weeks around each equinox, somewhere on the Earth, and passes through the Earth's shadow, 14$^\circ$ across at its distance, at every full Moon.

\subsection{The Moon's face}\label{the-moon-s-face}

The Moon's face is the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's: the Wide Angle Camera's color mosaic, toned to match a full-Moon photograph, and the Laser Altimeter's heights (NASA's CGI Moon Kit). The heights light the slopes, cast shadows toward the terminator, and shape the limb, so a zoomed crescent shows a ragged terminator and crater shadows, and the edge of the Moon in an eclipse breaks the last sunlight into Baily's beads. Five globes cover the Moon's history (Figure~\ref{fig:surfaces}). Tycho, about 109 million years old (\protect\hyperlink{cite.stoffler2001}{Stöffler \& Ryder 2001}, from the exposure ages of \protect\hyperlink{cite.arvidson1976}{Arvidson et al. 1976}), is taken away with its rays for the skies older than 109 Ma. Before about 3.9 Ga the maria had not been flooded: basalts filled the great basins from about 3.9 Ga and most of them by 3.3 Ga (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hiesinger2011}{Hiesinger et al. 2011}), so each mare darkens over 150 million years from an onset just before its oldest dated basalt. At 3.8 Ga Tranquillitatis is two-thirds dark, Serenitatis and Nectaris a third, and Imbrium and Procellarum are still bright: the familiar face is not there yet. At 4.0 Ga the near side is bright highland from limb to limb. At 3.8 Ga the eruptions themselves show: one to three fissure eruptions a lunation glow on the night side, a few to a hundred square kilometers of lava at about 1,400 K, as bright per area as the sunlit Moon and far redder, magnitude +3 to $-$1 as seen from the Earth.

The Moon of 4.5 Ga is not yet solid. Its magma ocean is open at the top, some ten million years after the impact (the site draws it so for the first 20 million years, then cools it into the Moon of 4.0 Ga by 4,450 Ma, its glow fading and its dark rafts graying as a crust closes over it; the times are a guess): rafts of dark quench crust and the first pale anorthosite, floating a few hundred meters to a kilometer and a half above the melt, broken by cracks and lava lakes at 1,400 to 1,600 K in four generations of floes, each a network of cracks within the last. The lava shines about as brightly per area as the sunlit Moon, so the disk glows orange by day and night alike, also inside the Earth's shadow, and the air around it carries a red aureole. The map and its relief are made up; that the young Moon looked like this is an inference from its magma ocean, not an observation.

The night side is drawn at a flat 6\% of the lit side's brightness rather than with a modeled earthshine, which the spectrum readout gives instead: about 2 $\times$ 10$^{-4}$ of the day side when the Earth is full from the Moon, and bluer.

\subsection{Moonlight}\label{moonlight}

Moonlight is the same sky model with the Moon in place of the Sun, scaled by the lunar phase (the Allen phase law with the opposition surge, as in \protect\hyperlink{cite.krisciunas1991}{Krisciunas \& Schaefer 1991}) and by the inverse square of the Moon's distance. It stays nearly the same in color from epoch to epoch but not in brightness: the full Moon lit the sky 2.2 times as brightly at 3.8 Ga and 57 times at 4.5 Ga, where it lit a 30-bar sky that, like its day sky, was a featureless peach glow.


\subsection{Lunar eclipses}\label{lunar-eclipses}

In a lunar eclipse the Moon passes through the Earth's shadow, which is lit only by sunlight bent into it through the Earth's limb: every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once. Its color and brightness are a measurement of the whole atmosphere at the terminator, which is why eclipses darkened after Tambora and El Chichón (\protect\hyperlink{cite.stothers2005}{Stothers 2005}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.keen1983}{Keen 1983}), why the eclipse of August 2008 carried the imprint of the small Kasatochi eruption (\protect\hyperlink{cite.garciamunoz2011}{García Muñoz et al. 2011}), and why the eclipsed Moon's spectrum is Earth's transmission spectrum as an exoplanet would show it (\protect\hyperlink{cite.palle2009}{Pallé et al. 2009}). The same physics, run on each epoch's air, gives the eclipse of every era.

The model (\emph{lunar\_eclipse.py}) traces rays from the Sun through the epoch's atmosphere at tangent heights from the ground to 120 km, bending with the refractivity of its gas mix and accumulating the optical depth of every component (gas, ozone, aerosol, sulfate, haze, soot) along the whole path, with today's cloud tops cutting the lowest rays. Seen from a point on the Moon, the Earth's limb at each position angle and height shows the Sun's limb-darkened disk wherever the ray's bending puts it; summing that over the ring of the limb (Link's method; \protect\hyperlink{cite.link1969}{Link 1969}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.garciamunoz2012}{García Muñoz et al. 2012} for a modern treatment) gives the spectrum of the light at each distance from the shadow's axis, and the direct sunlight past the top of the air gives the penumbra.

Today's umbra comes out a dull copper. A central eclipse puts the Moon 12.3 magnitudes below the full Moon, about V = $-$0.4, in the range observed for clear-air eclipses, with the center darkest and a paler, slightly bluish edge where ozone's Chappuis band removes the orange from the highest rays (the turquoise fringe of eclipse photographs). Before the Great Oxidation there is no ozone and that edge is orange too. A Tambora-sized sulfate veil puts the Moon near V = +11, a surface brightness of 27 mag/arcsec$^{2}$, far below the night sky: it vanishes, as it did to observers in June 1816 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.stothers2005}{Stothers 2005}). The K--Pg soot and the thick Neoarchean haze remove it entirely; the thin haze leaves a dim brown-red Moon near V = +5.

Two effects are new to the deep past. The closer Moon sat nearer the Earth than the refracted light comes to a focus (about 300,000 km today, past the Moon's 384,000), so the middle of the shadow is reached only by rays skimming the ground and goes dark: at 3.8 Ga the center of the shadow is a hundred million times fainter than its edge, a dark core in a glowing ring. And the 30-bar Hadean air is so refractive that below about 19 km its rays are trapped and run round the planet, while the upper air bends light strongly enough to fill the whole umbra of a Moon some 39 Earth radii out, an even, deep red at V = $-$1.7; but the Moon of 4.5 Ga, only 8 Earth radii out, sits well inside the focus, and at mid-eclipse it is gone, V = +12.

The shadow's geometry follows the distances and the young Sun's size. The umbra was 3.1 Moon widths across at 4.0 Ga against 2.7 today, and with twice as many full Moons a year, total lunar eclipses came nearly three times as often, while totality itself lasted at most 105 to 109 minutes in every epoch from 4.0 Ga on (Table~\ref{tab:lunar}). At 4.5 Ga the Moon, inside the Laplace radius of a fast-spinning Earth, orbits in the plane of the Earth's equator, its shadow is 3.6 Moon widths across and it races through it: around each equinox every full Moon is eclipsed, some 3,900 total eclipses a century, none longer than an hour. The rate also depends on the tilt of the Moon's orbit. It was a little steeper then: integrating today's 5.145$^\circ$ back along the distance history with the tidal model of \protect\hyperlink{cite.cuk2016}{Ćuk et al. (2016)}, in which Earth's tides flatten the orbit as the Moon recedes and tides raised in the Moon damp it, gives 5.46$^\circ$ at 2.7 Ga and 5.80$^\circ$ at 4.0 Ga (a little more for a Moon stiffer or more dissipative). The much larger tilts that model and \protect\hyperlink{cite.downey2022}{Downey, Nimmo \& Matsuyama (2023)} need for the young Moon belong to before the Cassini-state transition near 33 Earth radii, older than any epoch here but 4.5 Ga, whose Moon is in the equator's plane (\emph{lunar\_inclination.py}).

The ephemeris is checked against the almanac. For the total eclipses of 2022, 2025, 2026 and 2029, contact times fall within 2 to 7 minutes of the NASA catalog (\protect\hyperlink{cite.espenak2009}{Espenak \& Meeus 2009}) and umbral magnitudes within 0.04 (for 2029 June 26, 1.831 against 1.844); over 2000--2100 the page finds 144 umbral and 84 total eclipses, against the catalog's 142 and 85. On the page the Moon is drawn for an eye adapted to its brightest part, so the umbra looks black beside the penumbra in a partial phase and glows in totality, as it does to an observer.

\section{The planets and their moons}\label{planets}


\subsection{Where the planets were}\label{where-the-planets-were}

The five naked-eye planets, Uranus and Neptune are placed from JPL's approximate Keplerian elements (Standish's table, valid 1800--2050) for the date and time, light-time corrected, on the same magnitude scale as the stars (Meeus's formulae with the phase angle, Saturn's rings included when there are rings, and \protect\hyperlink{cite.mallama2018}{Mallama \& Hilton 2018} for Uranus and Neptune), and dimmed by the young Sun's fainter light: 0.42 magnitudes at 4.5 Ga, 0.24 at 2.7 Ga. Any planet brighter than magnitude $-$6 lights the sky as the Moon does. Within 10,000 years of today the positions are the real ones. Further off they cannot be known: the Solar System is chaotic, its uncertainties growing tenfold every ten million years or so (\protect\hyperlink{cite.laskar1989}{Laskar 1989}), and every epoch's age is uncertain by far more than any planet's period. So each planet is put at a random place on its orbit, the same for every visit to an epoch (and in the modern-era skies far from today, for each 100,000 years), and moves on with the date. The orbits' shapes are today's, known to some 50 million years and statistically before; their slow changes, which Standish's rates give for a few thousand years, are held where they are 3,000 years from today.

The two oldest skies have a different Solar System. In the reading of the meteorites' bombardment ages (\protect\hyperlink{cite.edwards2024}{Edwards et al. 2024}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.liu2022}{Liu, Raymond \& Jacobson 2022}), the giant planets went unstable early, within the first 100 million years; the 4.55 Ga sky shows them before it, in the best starting configuration of \protect\hyperlink{cite.nesvorny2012}{Nesvorný \& Morbidelli (2012)}: Jupiter at 5.6 AU and Saturn at 7.34 AU in a 3:2 resonance, a fifth giant at 9.62 AU, Uranus at 15.3 and Neptune at 20 AU, each in resonance with the next. The fifth giant, an ice giant like Neptune that Jupiter threw out of the Solar System in the instability (\protect\hyperlink{cite.nesvorny2011}{Nesvorný 2011}), is named here Oceanus. We date the instability to 4.48 Ga, the latest that the dated lunar and asteroidal impact record allows (\protect\hyperlink{cite.mojzsis2019}{Mojzsis et al. 2019}) and within the first 100 million years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.nesvorny2018}{Nesvorný et al. 2018}), so the 4.5 Ga sky still has the earlier arrangement; the bombardment ages would put it earlier still, as the 4.55 Ga sky's account says. From 4.48 Ga on the planets are on today's orbits.

Theia, the Mars-sized body whose collision with the proto-Earth made the Moon, is a wandering planet in the skies before the impact, 4.51 Ga. Its orbit is made up, as any that would meet the Earth's will do, since where it lay is not known (\protect\hyperlink{cite.branco2025}{Branco, Machado \& Raymond 2025}). Every 25 years or so it passes within a few hundredths of an AU, and at its closest it reaches magnitude $-$8.5, half lit, brighter than any planet has been since, lighting the night. Its face is invented too: an airless, cratered, iron-rich basaltic crust (\protect\hyperlink{cite.meier2014}{Meier, Reufer \& Wieler 2014}), its steam atmosphere lost to the young Sun within a few tens of millions of years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.odert2018}{Odert et al. 2018}), with eight great basins and a few young rayed craters.

The three largest asteroids are placed the same way, in every epoch: Vesta, which reaches magnitude 5.2 and the naked eye, Ceres and Pallas. Their elements are fit here to JPL Horizons over 1800--2200, with periodic terms in the mean longitude for Jupiter's pull (for Pallas, 0.9 degrees over 83 years), to a tenth or two of a degree as seen from the Sun, and their brightness follows the H, G system of \protect\hyperlink{cite.bowell1989}{Bowell et al. (1989)} with JPL's values.


\subsection{The planets close up}\label{the-planets-close-up}

Zooming in acts as a telescope, gaining 5 magnitudes against the sky for each tenfold zoom (magnification spreads the sky's light and leaves a point a point), so faint moons and Uranus and Neptune come out, and Mercury and Venus show by day. Each planet is a disk of its true angular size and flattening, on its IAU pole and prime meridian, lit from the Sun's side, so the phases of Mercury and Venus show; its limb darkens by a Minnaert law. Mercury and Mars wear spacecraft maps, MESSENGER's color mosaic and Viking's, toned toward true color, with their relief from MESSENGER (\protect\hyperlink{cite.becker2016}{Becker et al. 2016}) and the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter: slopes lit by the planet's own law, and rims and peaks hiding the Sun, so a gibbous Mars shows the shadows of Tharsis and Olympus Mons near its terminator and a half-lit Mercury a ragged, cratered one. Venus is cloud, cream and featureless, bright in the crescent. The moons of Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus, and Triton, have their own maps (Galileo, Voyager and Cassini mosaics), and cast their shadows on their planets, go dark in the planets' shadows, and pass behind and in front of them. Of Uranus's five, as of Triton, Voyager 2 saw only the southern halves, lit in 1986; the rest is filled in smooth, and since Uranus's north has turned toward the Sun and the Earth, it is mostly that blank half that faces us now. Uranus has its ten narrow rings, as \protect\hyperlink{cite.french2024}{French et al. (2024)} fit them to thirty years of stellar occultations: each the ellipse of its orbit, its periapse precessing (the $\varepsilon$ ring's 406 km off-center, turning once in nine months), its width growing from periapse to apoapse (the $\varepsilon$ ring's from 20 to 97 km) and its depth thinning to match, with optical depths after \protect\hyperlink{cite.esposito1991}{Esposito et al. (1991)}. The particles are as dark as charcoal, so even the $\varepsilon$ ring is only a faint thread at the deepest zoom, as it is in a real telescope; the dusty rings, a hundred-thousandth as thick, are left out. Ceres and Vesta wear Dawn's Framing Camera mosaics and stand on its shapes: each is drawn as the spheroid that fits its shape best (Ceres 482 by 446 km, matching \protect\hyperlink{cite.park2016}{Park et al. 2016}; Vesta 281 by 226 km), with what is left of the shape, up to 23 km on Vesta, as relief that lights the slopes and also bends the outline of the disk, so Vesta's south, cut by its great basins, shows flattened and lumpy. Phobos has the Mars Express map and shape the same way, though it is never more than a few pixels. Pallas, which no spacecraft has visited, is the spheroid of the shape from \protect\hyperlink{cite.marsset2020}{Marsset et al. (2020)}, in its own faintly blue tint. Figure~\ref{fig:surfaces} shows every rocky surface the site draws.

\subsection{Jupiter}\label{jupiter}

Jupiter close up is drawn, not mapped: its clouds change too fast for any one map to stand for an epoch. The bands lie where the Cassini wind profile (\protect\hyperlink{cite.porco2003}{Porco et al. 2003}) puts its jets, and the clouds are a tracer stirred by eddies: each point is followed back along the streamlines of a stream function of noise, three octaves of cells three times longer along the winds than across, renewing at different times, its strength greatest in the belts and toward the poles and coming and going in storm patches some 45$^\circ$ long. The flow keeps area, so the belts and zones and the finer banding within them marble into curls and filaments rather than creases, and the jets' own shear draws them out along the bands. Along each boundary between a belt and a zone, Kelvin--Helmholtz billows roll up, turning the way of the shear there. Dark festoons trail from the North Equatorial Belt into the Equatorial Zone with blue-gray hot spots at their heads; dark barges sit on the belt's northern edge, and white ovals in the South South Temperate Belt. The tracer approach follows the flow-field techniques of \protect\hyperlink{cite.dziewanowski_flowfields}{Dziewanowski (2024)}.

The Great Red Spot is a vortex in the same flow: an anticyclone turning the clouds along its ellipses, faster toward its edge, with a slight inflow that draws belt and zone onto its rim as spiral arms, and its turbulent wake to the west. Its history is the observed one. It was first seen for sure in 1831 (Cassini's ``permanent spot'' of 1665--1713 may have been another storm), so the 1816 sky has none. Its longitude follows the measured record (Peek's for 1831--1952, JUPOS since), and its size the fits of \protect\hyperlink{cite.simon2018}{Simon et al. (2018)} from 1979, 405.0 $-$ 0.1939 Y degrees long and 107.2 $-$ 0.0483 Y wide, and before 1979 a steady shrinking from about 35$^\circ$ long in the 1880s. The fits meet in a round spot 8.4$^\circ$ across in 2045, and it is held there for 2100: whether it shrinks on, steadies or fades is not known. It was reddest in 1879--1882 and has deepened again since 2014. In the deep past there is no Red Spot: storms like it come and go over centuries.

\subsection{Saturn and its rings}\label{saturn-and-its-rings}

Saturn close up is drawn as Jupiter is, a tracer stirred by the same flow, with its own winds, bands and colors. The winds are Cassini's at the cloud tops in 2004--2009 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.garciamelendo2011}{García-Melendo et al. 2011}), fit by twenty Gaussian jets to 6 m/s rms in System III, which also turns the planet: the broad equatorial jet near 400 m/s, and narrower jets alternating with latitude toward the poles. The belts lie where the wind falls off poleward, as Jupiter's do, the broad pair at 18--32$^\circ$ and fainter, narrower ones beyond. Saturn's haze mutes all of it, so the eddies stir about half as hard as Jupiter's, the billows turn less, and the hues vary a half to a third as much. Around the north pole the jet at 78$^\circ$N meanders in a standing wave of six, the hexagon (\protect\hyperlink{cite.godfrey1988}{Godfrey 1988}), golden inside in the northern summer and bluish gray in the winter; both poles have a vortex with a dark eye and a bright wall, a thin ribbon wave runs along the jet at 47$^\circ$N, and small bright storms rise in Storm Alley near 40$^\circ$S. The winter hemisphere, out of the Sun, shows bluish at any size, as Cassini saw the north before 2009. That change lags the season by several years, and the lag is not drawn.

Saturn's rings are drawn from the Cassini radio occultation of 3 May 2005 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.marouf_corss}{Marouf et al. n.d.}), its optical depth at 10 km resolution from the C ring to the outer A ring, corrected to visible light in the C ring, the Cassini division and the outer A ring by the same occultation's Ka band, which sees the sub-centimeter particles the X band misses (in the outer A ring \protect\hyperlink{cite.jerousek2016}{Jerousek et al. 2016} find them too), with the F ring added. Each ring is a single-scattering layer (as \protect\hyperlink{cite.cuzzi1984}{Cuzzi et al. 1984}): lit from the Sun's side it shines by its depth and particle brightness, the A ring 0.7 of the B ring's and the C ring and Cassini division half of it and grayer; seen from the unlit side only light passing through it shows, so the B ring is black and the C ring and Cassini division glow. The rings shadow the globe, and the globe shadows the rings with its oblate outline and a penumbra the Sun's width. Near the equinoxes, when the Sun is low over the rings, they shadow the moons too: a moon passing behind them is banded by their shadow and dimmed by what they let through, Mimas by up to 4 magnitudes deep in the B ring's. Edge-on, the rings thin below a pixel.

The rings are young. How young is still argued, from tens to a few hundred million years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.iess2019}{Iess et al. 2019}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.kempf2023}{Kempf et al. 2023}), and we adopt the account of \protect\hyperlink{cite.wisdom2022}{Wisdom et al. (2022)}: an icy moon the size of Iapetus, named Chrysalis, orbiting between Titan and Iapetus in a 3:1 resonance with Titan, was torn apart roughly 100 to 200 million years ago, about 160 million years ago in the case they illustrate, freeing Saturn's tilt from Neptune's pull and making the rings of its debris. We draw that illustrated case, so the rings appear 160 million years ago, and before them Chrysalis is a moon of magnitude 13 to 14 beside a ringless Saturn, from 300 million years back. Younger rings are drawn more massive and cleaner: their depth grows with youth as age$^{-0.75}$, between ring-rain losses (\protect\hyperlink{cite.odonoghue2019}{O'Donoghue et al. 2019}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.waite2018}{Waite et al. 2018}) and viscous spreading (\protect\hyperlink{cite.salmon2010}{Salmon et al. 2010}), half again today's at 66 Ma, and the meteoroid darkening of the C ring and Cassini division (\protect\hyperlink{cite.cuzzi1998}{Cuzzi \& Estrada 1998}) is two fifths of today's. Both are guesses. Saturn's pole precesses once in about 1.87 million years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.ward2004}{Ward \& Hamilton 2004}), so the rings open from a different direction at each time: turned by the known rate over the last ten million years, at random before.


\subsection{Neptune's clouds}\label{neptune-s-clouds}

Neptune close up is drawn the same way, a tracer in its own flow. The winds are Voyager 2's cloud-tracked profile (\protect\hyperlink{cite.sromovsky1993}{Sromovsky et al. 1993}): retrograde at 400 m/s at the equator, prograde at 250 m/s near 70$^\circ$, in System III, the 16.11-hour radio period that also turns the planet. Its deck is smooth, so the eddies stir it only lightly and no billows roll up between bands. Its color is the true one, a pale greenish blue only a little bluer than Uranus's (\protect\hyperlink{cite.irwin2024}{Irwin et al. 2024}), not the deep blue of Voyager's contrast-stretched images. On it are bright clouds of methane ice, in streaks drawn out by the shear of the winds that carry them, at southern mid-latitudes, near 30$^\circ$N and near 70$^\circ$S, each lasting some days; and the dark wave circling near 60$^\circ$S, wandering a few degrees in latitude and drifting 4.87$^\circ$ a day in System III, with a bright band just north of it (\protect\hyperlink{cite.karkoschka2011}{Karkoschka 2011}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.chavez2023}{Chavez et al. 2023}). Dark vortices come and go, a new one every four to six years, each lasting about two (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hsu2019}{Hsu et al. 2019}): deep-blue ovals with bright methane clouds along their poleward edges, turning as anticyclones and drifting with the wind at their latitude. The Great Dark Spot that Voyager 2 found is drawn as it was, at 22$^\circ$S, 13,000 by 6,600 km, from late 1988 until it was gone in 1994 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.smith1989}{Smith et al. 1989}). At other times a vortex is drawn at random as their rates and sizes have it, since where one was on any given day is not known.


\subsection{The giants when young}\label{the-giants-when-young}

The giant planets were warmer when young, and their clouds were different. Each cools as it ages: we take its intrinsic temperature, the effective temperature its own heat alone would give it, to fall as the age to the $-$0.29, a power-law fit to the cooling tracks of \protect\hyperlink{cite.burrows1997}{Burrows et al. (1997)}, scaled to today's (98 K for Jupiter, 78 K for Saturn, 53 K for Neptune, from the effective temperatures Voyager measured, \protect\hyperlink{cite.hanel1981}{Hanel et al. 1981}, 1983 and \protect\hyperlink{cite.pearl1991}{Pearl \& Conrath 1991}, less the sunlight's share), and add the young Sun's light at each planet's distance. For planets of these masses the hot and cold starts of formation meet within a few tens of millions of years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.spiegel2012}{Spiegel \& Burrows 2012}), so the history is surest over the last four billion years and least sure in the oldest sky. Then Jupiter's effective temperature was near 500 K in the 4.55 Ga sky, 330 K in the 4.5 Ga sky, 185 K at 4.0 Ga and 170 K at 3.8 Ga, against 124 K now; Saturn's 395, 265, 145 and 135 K, against 95; Neptune's 270, 180, 98 and 90 K, against 59. Uranus is drawn as it is now: it gives out too little heat for any model that starts it hot (\protect\hyperlink{cite.fortney2011}{Fortney et al. 2011}), and how warm it was is not known.

A cloud deck forms where the air is cold enough for its gas to condense, and the coldest air, at the top of the troposphere, is near 0.85 of the effective temperature, so as each planet cooled its clouds formed in turn from the bottom up; young, Jupiter and Saturn had no ammonia clouds and a top deck of water clouds, and the ice giants' clouds lay higher (\protect\hyperlink{cite.fortney2011}{Fortney et al. 2011}). We draw three stages. Above about 165 K Jupiter and Saturn have no ammonia ice on top and show the NH4SH haze beneath, paler and less colored, as Jupiter is at 4.0 and 3.8 Ga; above about 85 K Neptune has no methane ice, no bright streaks, and shows its H2S deck higher up, as pale a cyan as Uranus, whose top deck is H2S now (\protect\hyperlink{cite.irwin2018}{Irwin et al. 2018}), as Neptune is at 4.0 and 3.8 Ga. Warmer still, above about 230 K for the gas giants and 155 K for the ice giants, the top is a high deck of NH4SH and water clouds, bright and near white, Sudarsky et al.'s (2000) class II: Jupiter and Saturn in the 4.5 Ga sky and the ice giants in both of the oldest, their bands showing more in brightness than in hue, the ice giants' storms of moist convection rising bright out of their mid-latitudes. Above about 380 K water no longer condenses high up and the planet is clear, their class III, a dark azure of Rayleigh scattering and methane absorption: Jupiter in the 4.55 Ga sky, and most of Saturn. Each planet's point is tinted and brightened or dimmed with its disk, and its spectrum's reflectance mixed toward the stage's: flatter and gray for the NH4SH and water decks, with shallower methane bands above the higher clouds, and blue and dark in the red, with deep methane bands, when clear.

Oceanus, of about Neptune's mass, cools as Neptune does; nearer the Sun, at 9.6 AU, its effective temperature is 270 K in the 4.55 Ga sky and 180 K in the 4.5 Ga sky, so in both it has the high bright deck of NH4SH and water clouds, a pale banded cyan. Its bands are laid out as the ice giants' circulation runs, air rising at mid-latitudes, where clouds are thick and bright, and sinking at the equator and poles, where they are thin (\protect\hyperlink{cite.fletcher2020}{Fletcher et al. 2020}): a broad darker equatorial belt, bright zones from about 18$^\circ$ to 45$^\circ$, narrower belts near 50$^\circ$ and darker polar caps, carried and stirred as Neptune's are, in Neptune's winds, with bright storm tops in the rising zones of both hemispheres. Its day, unknown, is taken as 16 hours. Through the tens of bars of CO$_2$ of the 4.5 Ga sky none of this can be seen from the ground.

\subsection{The planets' moons}\label{the-planets-moons}

The moons with fitted orbits, fitted here to JPL Horizons over 1980--2060, are Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto; Mimas, Enceladus, Tethys, Dione, Rhea, Titan and Iapetus; Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania and Oberon; Triton; and Mars's Phobos and Deimos. Most hold to a tenth of a degree. Iapetus's two faces show: its trailing side is ten times brighter than its leading side, so it is magnitude 10.2 at western elongation and 11.9 at eastern. The planets' moons also change with the time. Triton, retrograde, was captured in the giant-planet instability, so the skies before 4.48 Ga lack it, and Neptune has instead a family of regular moons, here named Kymo, Galene, Thetis and Amphitrite, drawn like Uranus's four largest at the same distances: \protect\hyperlink{cite.rufu2017}{Rufu \& Canup (2017)} find that a system no heavier than Uranus's fits best, and that capturing Triton scattered and destroyed it. Mars, from 4.3 to 3.5 Ga, has Harmonia (named here), the moon of some twenty times Phobos's mass at 3.3 Mars radii that \protect\hyperlink{cite.cuk2020}{Ćuk et al. (2020)} find tilted Deimos's orbit, one generation of Mars's cycle of rings and moons (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hesselbrock2017}{Hesselbrock \& Minton 2017}). Phobos is falling toward Mars: its tidal acceleration, half the rate its mean motion grows, is 1.258 $\times$ 10$^{-3}$ degrees per year squared (\protect\hyperlink{cite.brozovic2025}{Brozović, Jacobson \& Park 2025}), which is put back into its fitted longitude, so its place stays right to a few hundredths of a degree across centuries rather than drifting by degrees. Back in time, with Mars's tidal lag held fixed, its orbit grows as the 2/13 power of 1 + t/38 Myr, to 3.2 Mars radii at 66 Ma against 2.77 now; Deimos, beyond the synchronous orbit, was only some 130 km nearer at 3.8 Ga. Both of Phobos's histories fit its orbit (\protect\hyperlink{cite.cuk2025}{Ćuk, Anand \& Minton 2025}): formed early just inside the synchronous orbit, or about 100 million years ago at the Roche limit as the latest moon of the ring cycle. The site follows the second, as its Harmonia does, so Phobos appears only in the skies of the last 100 million years, and Deimos from 4.3 Ga on.

\section{The stars and the Galaxy}\label{stars}


\subsection{The stars moved back}\label{the-stars-moved-back}

Today's sky has the thousand brightest stars of the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hoffleit1991}{Hoffleit \& Warren 1991}), down to about magnitude 4.6, carried to the year shown by their motion and precession, each tinted by its color temperature and shown only when it is brighter than the naked-eye limit for the sky around it, so stars come out one by one in twilight and cities keep only the brightest. The Earth's axis turns by the long-term precession of \protect\hyperlink{cite.vondrak2011}{Vondrák, Capitaine \& Wallace (2011)}, good to a degree or so over 200,000 years either way: Polaris is the pole star only for a few thousand years, and Vega takes its place 12,000 years from now. Each epoch's own year has today's axis, since its phase long ago cannot be pinned down; beyond 200,000 years from it the axis turns on at the same rate and its tilt rocks between 22$^\circ$ and 24.5$^\circ$ on the 41,000-year cycle. Going back, the stars move, and how far the site can follow them sets two kinds of sky.

Over the last two million years, and the 250,000 ahead, every catalog star moves in a straight line from its Hipparcos parallax, proper motion and radial velocity (the XHIP compilation of \protect\hyperlink{cite.anderson2012}{Anderson \& Francis 2012}), brightening or fading as its distance changes, and the site shows the thousand brightest of the year: today's thousand and 732 others that were among them at some time over that span. Over two million years the Galaxy's pull bends the stars' paths by only a few parsecs. The sky is recognizable but rearranged. At 1.78 Ma Canopus is the brightest star, $-$1.6, and Sirius, far away then, is fourth magnitude; at 342 ka Aldebaran, near its closest pass, outshines everything at $-$1.5, and Sirius is +0.8; Sirius itself is brightest, about $-$1.6, some 60,000 years from now. Constellation figures move with their stars, and a line is dropped once its stars have drifted three times today's distance apart.

Further back the lines no longer hold, and none of today's neighbors was near the Sun. Between 1.9 and 2.2 million years ago, at a time of its own, each real star fades out over 50,000 years and gives way to stand-ins of its kind: stars of its absolute magnitude, color and spectral type passing the Sun one after another on straight lines at 15 to 40 km/s, as the nearby stars' speeds relative to the Sun run, each nearest the Sun at today's star's distance and so as bright as it, toward the galactic latitude it has today and at a random longitude. Each passage lasts the time it takes to go twice that distance, from half a million to twenty million years, and fades into the next, so the deep sky changes as fast as the nearby stars move and never all at once. The stand-ins' labels give the type they copy, not a name. The sky's statistics are right; its patterns are invented, as they would be for any reconstruction that far back. The stars that outshone Sirius in the last ten million years (below) are traced through the Galaxy for the report; on the site, those of the last two million years show at their brightest.


\subsection{Stars brighter than Sirius}\label{stars-brighter-than-sirius}

Sirius, at magnitude $-$1.46, is the brightest star in today's night sky, but it has not always been. The Sun's neighbors drift past it at tens of kilometers a second, so a star a hundred parsecs away now may have passed within a few parsecs of it a few million years ago. Tracing every Hipparcos star that has a radial velocity back 10 Myr through the Galaxy, with 2,000 draws over each star's measurement errors, finds eleven that outshone today's Sirius (Table~\ref{tab:encounters}). The brightest is $\theta$ Columbae, a fifth-magnitude star today, which passed about 2 pc from the Sun 4.7 million years ago and peaked near magnitude $-$5, as bright as Venus at its best; Gaia DR3 astrometry (\protect\hyperlink{cite.gaia2023}{Gaia Collaboration 2023}) gives the same ($-$5.2 at 2.1 pc, 5.1 million years ago). $\varepsilon$ Canis Majoris reached $-$4.35 4.4 million years ago (its spell as the brightest star has been known since \protect\hyperlink{cite.tomkin1998}{Tomkin 1998}, who gave $-$4.0 at 4.7 million years), and $\beta$ Canis Majoris $-$3.6 at the same time. From about 3.5 to 5 million years ago the night sky held at least two stars brighter than any star today, and at times three.

These stars now lie in Columba, Canis Major and Puppis because that is the solar antapex: the Sun moves toward Hercules at about 20 km/s relative to its neighbors, so the stars it has passed end up behind it. The closest pass, $\zeta$ Leporis at 1.3 pc 850,000 years ago, came as close as $\alpha$ Centauri is now, far too distant to disturb the planets. Algol's radial velocity (3.7 $\pm$ 3.9 km/s, hard to measure in an eclipsing binary) is too uncertain to say whether it passed within 1.3 or 28 pc of the Sun. Rigel, Saiph, Mintaka, Alnitak and $\xi$ Persei also come out bright, but 6 to 10 million years ago, about their own ages, so they are left out. The older peaks fall before then, in its stand-in sky.


\subsection{The Milky Way, nebulae and galaxies}\label{the-milky-way-nebulae-and-galaxies}

The Milky Way is a model in galactic coordinates rather than a photograph: a disk and bulge, the Sagittarius and Scutum star clouds, the Great Rift from Cygnus to Sagittarius, the Coalsack and the Ophiuchus and Taurus dust, peaking at 20.2 mag/arcsec$^{2}$ in the Sagittarius star cloud (\protect\hyperlink{cite.leinert1998}{Leinert et al. 1998}), and shown only in a dark sky. Its color is that of its stars: old ones, and young ones in a layer a few degrees thick that is richest along the spiral arms seen end on, in Cygnus and Carina, and absent from the bulge, all reddened by the band's own dust (\protect\hyperlink{cite.cardelli1989}{Cardelli, Clayton \& Mathis 1989}), so Cygnus is grayer and the edges of the Great Rift and the Coalsack browner. In the older skies its brightest part lies where the Galactic center then lay as seen from the Sun, from the Sun's orbit traced back through the same model Galaxy, and the band shifts by a few degrees with the Sun's height above the plane: at 66 Ma the Sun was 100 pc north of it. Beyond about a billion years the Sun's place on its orbit is only nominal. The Galaxy itself changes too. The Sun was born about 5 kpc from the center and has since migrated out to 8.2 kpc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.frankel2018}{Frankel et al. 2018}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.lu2024}{Lu et al. 2024}); the disc grew from the inside out, its half-mass radius by 43\% over 7 Gyr (\protect\hyperlink{cite.frankel2019}{Frankel et al. 2019}); its stars were younger, so the disc was thinner and bluer and its bulge less faded, following the star formation history measured from Gaia (\protect\hyperlink{cite.snaith2014}{Snaith et al. 2014}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.mor2019}{Mor et al. 2019}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.gallart2024}{Gallart et al. 2024}); and there was more gas, so more dust (\protect\hyperlink{cite.genzel2015}{Genzel et al. 2015}). Seen from the inner disc 4.5 Ga, the band was little brighter along the plane, where the extra dust hid the extra stars, but it was broader, its glow a magnitude brighter 20$^\circ$ from the plane, and browner, the bulge behind more dust. Dark clouds last only tens of millions of years and spiral arms come and go, so before 30 Ma the band's rifts, star clouds and young-star fields are stand-ins of the same kind, not today's.

The 34 nebulae and galaxies that binoculars show under a dark sky are drawn from survey photographs, their stars taken out, each scaled to its catalog magnitude and surface brightness, and turning into its photograph when zoomed. They change with the epoch. The Andromeda and Triangulum galaxies fall toward the Milky Way on a timing-argument orbit (\protect\hyperlink{cite.vandermarel2012}{van der Marel et al. 2012}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.kahn1959}{Kahn \& Woltjer 1959}), so at 4.5 Ga Andromeda is three quarters of today's size. The Large Magellanic Cloud is on its first infall (\protect\hyperlink{cite.kallivayalil2013}{Kallivayalil et al. 2013}), traced back through the Galaxy's halo with dynamical friction: a small patch in another part of the sky in the Hadean, larger than today's at 66 Ma. Galaxies beyond the Local Group were nearer by the cosmic scale factor. The nebulae are young: each is drawn only within its age, so the Orion Nebula is absent at 1.78 Ma, and none of today's shine at 66 Ma or before, when others, unknowable, did.


\subsection{Events on their dates}\label{events}

The timeline also holds the sky's own dated events, which come on their days in whichever epoch's sky holds that day, and which the date picker lists under Extended events: every supernova, nova and stellar eruption known to have shone brighter than magnitude 3 (Table~\ref{tab:transients}), the 24 real comets at their best apparitions, three volcanic veils, three great aurorae, three meteor storms and three impacts, and four events to come. None is an epoch of its own: each is laid over the sky of its time, the volcanic veils on its air, the rest among its stars, meteors and aurorae. Going to one sets the clock to the hour it shows best, highest in the darkest sky (a meteor storm at its peak, a volcanic veil at dusk, an impact as it comes in), and the latitude it is best seen from: of the equator and the two mid-latitudes, where most people live, the one that sees it highest in the darkest sky, and a polar one only when none of those sees it 10$^\circ$ up at night.


\subsection{Supernovae, novae and eruptions}\label{supernovae}

Every supernova, nova and stellar eruption known to have outshone magnitude 3 appears on its date (Table~\ref{tab:transients}): the supernovae of history from SN 185 to SN 1987A; those before history, dated by their remnants and pulsars, from a hypothetical one about 20 pc away at the end of the Devonian, 359 Ma, which may have stripped the ozone layer (\protect\hyperlink{cite.fields2020}{Fields et al. 2020}), through the two whose iron-60 lies in the deep-sea crust (\protect\hyperlink{cite.wallner2016}{Wallner et al. 2016}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.breitschwerdt2016}{Breitschwerdt et al. 2016}), to Vela Jr's a few thousand years ago; fifteen classical novae from 1848 to 1999 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.duerbeck1987}{Duerbeck 1987}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.strope2010}{Strope, Schaefer \& Henden 2010}); and three eruptions: P Cygni's of 1600 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.degroot1969}{de Groot 1969}), CK Vulpeculae's of 1670, perhaps two stars merging (\protect\hyperlink{cite.shara1985}{Shara, Moffat \& Webbink 1985}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.kaminski2015}{Kamiński et al. 2015}), and $\eta$ Carinae's Great Eruption, second only to Sirius in 1843 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.smith2011}{Smith \& Frew 2011}). A record's date is the day it was first seen, converted from the Julian calendar; one before history has a day chosen in the year its remnant's or pulsar's age gives, and where its place in the sky is not known it is assumed, as the table says. The remnants give the rest: SN 185's is RCW 86, a Type Ia's at 2.5 kpc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.williams2011}{Williams et al. 2011}), and SN 1181's Pa 30 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.ritter2021}{Ritter et al. 2021}); the Monogem Ring is about 86,000 years old at 288 pc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.thorsett2003}{Thorsett et al. 2003}), the Cygnus Loop 10,000 to 20,000 at 735 pc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.fesen2018}{Fesen et al. 2018}), the Vela pulsar 11,000 at 287 pc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.dodson2003}{Dodson et al. 2003}), Puppis A 4,400 by its neutron star's motion at 1.3 kpc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.becker2012}{Becker et al. 2012}), and Vela Jr 2,400 to 5,100 at about 750 pc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.allen2015}{Allen et al. 2015}). The peaks are the records', or for a supernova without one a Type II-P's absolute magnitude, $-$16.75 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.richardson2014}{Richardson et al. 2014}), at its remnant's distance: SN 1006 reached about $-$7.5 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.winkler2003}{Winkler, Gupta \& Long 2003}), Tycho's $-$4 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.krause2008}{Krause et al. 2008}). Each follows a light curve of its kind: a Type II-P's two weeks' rise, three months' plateau and radioactive tail (\protect\hyperlink{cite.anderson2014}{Anderson et al. 2014}); a Type Ia's quicker rise and fall (\protect\hyperlink{cite.phillips1993}{Phillips 1993}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.leibundgut2000}{Leibundgut 2000}); a stripped star's Type Ib/c (\protect\hyperlink{cite.drout2011}{Drout et al. 2011}); a nova's rise of a day or two, its fall by two and three magnitudes in its own times and a power-law tail (\protect\hyperlink{cite.strope2010}{Strope, Schaefer \& Henden 2010}), or the measured curve where it was odd, as RR Pictoris's slow rise and DQ Herculis's plunge into its own dust; and SN 1987A's slow climb to 2.9 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hamuy1990}{Hamuy \& Suntzeff 1990}). Each has a spectrum of its kind in the readout, P Cygni lines from a supernova's ejecta, broad Balmer and Fe II emission for a nova. The fainter are drawn as stars; the bright ones with a glare that grows from magnitude $-$2 to $-$9, and the brightest shown lights the ground.

Two epochs are built round a supernova. Geminga's, about 342,000 years ago by its pulsar's spin-down age (\protect\hyperlink{cite.salvati2008}{Salvati \& Sacco 2008}), exploded 90 to 240 pc away toward Orion (\protect\hyperlink{cite.pellizza2005}{Pellizza et al. 2005}); a typical Type II-P peaks near absolute magnitude $-$16.75 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.richardson2014}{Richardson et al. 2014}), so from 147 pc it reached about $-$11, a fifth of the full Moon's light from a single point, bright enough to show by day and cast shadows at night for the three months of its plateau. Each follows a Type II-P light curve: two weeks' rise, the plateau, a drop of a few magnitudes after about 100 to 130 days, and the radioactive tail, fading a magnitude every hundred days, so it is gone to the eye in a few years. The $\zeta$ Ophiuchi supernova, 1.78 million years ago at 107 pc in the Scorpius--Centaurus association (\protect\hyperlink{cite.neuhauser2019}{Neuhäuser et al. 2020}), unbound its companion, now the runaway star $\zeta$ Oph, and peaked near $-$11.6, twice as bright. Its spectrum readout is a Type II-P near peak, with P Cygni profiles from fast ejecta (\protect\hyperlink{cite.filippenko1997}{Filippenko 1997}). At these distances neither touches the sky's colors (\protect\hyperlink{cite.thomas2016}{Thomas et al. 2016}).

Four are to come. Betelgeuse's supernova is the same event as Geminga's, in the same patch of sky: 1.4$^\circ$ from Geminga's star in the sky of 342 ka, a red supergiant of 16.5 to 19 solar masses burning helium in its core, some 100,000 years from collapse, at 168 pc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.joyce2020}{Joyce et al. 2020}), or 222 pc by radio astrometry (\protect\hyperlink{cite.harper2017}{Harper et al. 2017}). A red supergiant ends as a Type II-P, so it should peak at $-$10 to $-$10.6, close to Geminga's $-$11, and the Geminga sky is a fair picture of Orion's own to come; the site sets it, as an assumption, in AD 100,000. The others are tidal disruptions, stars torn apart by a black hole. The Large Magellanic Cloud's black hole, of about 6$\times$10$^{5}$ solar masses by the hypervelocity stars it has thrown into the Milky Way's halo (\protect\hyperlink{cite.han2025}{Han et al. 2025}), sits where \protect\hyperlink{cite.chisholm2026}{Chisholm et al. (2026)} predict; a Sun-like star's debris would fall back on it at about 140 times the Eddington rate, so the light holds near the Eddington luminosity, and at the Cloud's 49.6 kpc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.pietrzynski2019}{Pietrzyński et al. 2019}) it peaks near V = $-$0.6, as bright as Canopus for months, and stays visible for about thirty years. The rate is uncertain: with no nuclear star cluster the Cloud would see one in 10$^{5}$ to 10$^{6}$ years (\protect\hyperlink{cite.pfister2020}{Pfister et al. 2020}), but it has thrown out about as many hypervelocity stars as the Milky Way's center, whose disruptions run near one in 10$^{4}$; at one in 33,000 years the median wait puts it in AD 25,000. Andromeda's black hole, 1.4$\times$10$^{8}$ solar masses (\protect\hyperlink{cite.bender2005}{Bender et al. 2005}), can tear a Sun-like star apart only on a deep plunge round a fast spin, perhaps once in 10$^{5}$ to 10$^{6}$ years; the debris returns over about 300 days near the Eddington rate, and at 765 kpc it makes a first-magnitude star in M31's core for most of a year, visible for over a decade, set at the median wait in AD 210,000. Both are predictions, their dates assumed.

\section{The night and what crosses it}\label{night}


\subsection{Twilight and the night sky}\label{twilight-and-the-night-sky}

Because the photometric scale of Section~\ref{method} is absolute, the night is drawn on the same scale as the day. The sky is computed for Sun heights down to 20$^\circ$ below the horizon; past 9$^\circ$ below, where the model's twilight fades too slowly, we make it dim by about a magnitude per degree, as measured. Without the Sun or Moon the sky has its own light: today 22.0 mag/arcsec$^{2}$ at a dark zenith (\protect\hyperlink{cite.leinert1998}{Leinert et al. 1998}), half of it starlight and zodiacal light and half airglow, brighter toward the horizon.

Nearly all of today's airglow needs free oxygen: the 557.7 nm green and 630 nm red lines of oxygen atoms, the hydroxyl bands (H + O$_3$), the sodium D lines cycled through ozone, and the NO$_2$ continuum. We take its strength from the air's O$_2$: 30\% of today's at 1\% of today's oxygen (2.2 Ga), where few atoms are left near 95 km, 80\% at 10\% (the Snowball and the Cryogenian, 169 DU of ozone), 90\% at 81\% (466 Ma), today's from today's level up, and log-linearly between, so it fades in through the Great Oxidation. Before it the oxygen airglow is gone, replaced by a faint violet glow of the O$_2$ Herzberg II bands from oxygen split off CO$_2$, as on Venus (\protect\hyperlink{cite.krasnopolsky1983}{Krasnopolsky 1983}): about as many photons as today's airglow but only 15\% of its luminance, fading out as O$_2$ rises from a ten-thousandth to a hundredth of today's. So Archean and Hadean nights were darker and bluer. These strengths are estimates; no model of an anoxic nightglow exists.

Starlight, the Milky Way and the airglow all come from above the air and are dimmed by it, at the rate given in Section~\ref{method}, 1.086 times the air's optical depth at 550 nm: 0.24 magnitude per airmass in today's clean air, 0.18 to 0.21 in the clean, ozone-poor air of 3.8 to 0.7 Ga, 0.84 under the thick haze, 0.79 in a polluted city, 0.87 under the proto-Earth's guessed air and dust, 2.47 through the impact-winter soot, and 5.8 under 30 bar of CO$_2$. That alone would leave the brightest stars at fourth magnitude overhead, but the bombardment's zodiacal glow, two thousand times today's, hides every one.


\subsection{Aurorae}\label{aurorae}

The aurora is an oval round the magnetic pole, fixed relative to the Sun: on a moderately active night today (Kp 3) it spans about 63--72$^\circ$ latitude on the night side (the ovals of \protect\hyperlink{cite.feldstein1967}{Feldstein \& Starkov 1967}). Its field lines reach out to the magnetopause, whose distance goes as the sixth root of the field's pressure over the solar wind's. At 3.45 Ga a field half to 70\% of today's and the young Sun's dense wind held the magnetopause at about half today's distance (\protect\hyperlink{cite.tarduno2010}{Tarduno et al. 2010}), which brings the oval's edge down to about 50$^\circ$ even in quiet times, and the young Sun's frequent flares and mass ejections (\protect\hyperlink{cite.airapetian2016}{Airapetian et al. 2016}) make storms the usual state. Through the Proterozoic the wind weakens (\protect\hyperlink{cite.wood2005}{Wood et al. 2005}) and the oval retreats toward the pole.

The colors follow the air. Today's green and red lines are oxygen atoms. At 2.2 Ga they are about a third of today's. With no free oxygen the aurora is nitrogen's: violet N$_2^{+}$ bands and a pink border of N$_2$ first positive bands where faster electrons reach lowest, brighter without oxygen to quench them, with a weak green line from CO$_2$ split by the electrons, as Perseverance saw on Mars (\protect\hyperlink{cite.knutsen2025}{Knutsen et al. 2025}). The 30-bar Hadean air is mostly CO$_2$, so there the green line would be stronger and the nitrogen faint, but the air, at 5.8 magnitudes of extinction per airmass, and the bombardment's zodiacal glow hide the aurora altogether. Brightness is for a dark-adapted eye, which sees violet far better than the deep red. The ancient line strengths are estimates.

Each night has its own activity about the epoch's typical level, and the oval is drawn at it. Today that is Kp 3; the young Sun's typical night is far stormier, Kp 6 at 4.0 Ga, and its oval reaches correspondingly lower, to about 37$^\circ$ on the night side at 4.0 Ga and 43$^\circ$ at 3.8 Ga (Table~\ref{tab:phenomena} gives both). The arcs are thin sheets along the field lines, green below and red above, with field-aligned rays converging overhead on the magnetic zenith; they fold, drift and break up in two substorms a night near midnight. A storm of the class of September 1859, when aurora stood overhead near 40$^\circ$ magnetic latitude, brings today's oval down to about 45$^\circ$ and the young Earth's to the equator. Three great storms on record come on their dates in the modern-era skies, each drawn as that class of storm: the Carrington storms of late August and early September 1859, with aurora overhead in the Caribbean (\protect\hyperlink{cite.green2006}{Green \& Boardsen 2006}); the storm of 13 to 15 May 1921, seen to Samoa and Jamaica (\protect\hyperlink{cite.silverman2001}{Silverman \& Cliver 2001}); and the storm of 10 and 11 May 2024, red aurora over Mexico and Florida, the strongest since 2003 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.hayakawa2025}{Hayakawa et al. 2025}).

\subsection{Meteors and meteor showers}\label{meteors-and-meteor-showers}

Each meteor is a meteoroid entering the air along a straight path 80 to 125 km up, with its brightness, size and speed tied by the photographic relation of \protect\hyperlink{cite.jacchia1967}{Jacchia, Verniani \& Briggs (1967)} and its count set by the measured fireball rate. Today a dark site sees about 8 an hour, more before dawn. A meteor shows only when brighter than the sky around it, so twilight, moonlight and city light thin them out. The air enters three ways. Bright, fast meteors leave persistent trains that glow green and then orange; that chemistry needs oxygen and ozone, so there are no trains before the Great Oxidation. Each meteor's color comes from a spectrum built for its speed and the epoch's air: the metals of the meteoroid, a hotter component that grows with speed, and the air's own emission, oxygen and nitrogen today or carbon monoxide bands in a CO$_2$-rich air, which no one has measured. And in the Early Hadean's 30 bar every density level sits higher, so meteors burn about 22 km higher up.

The rate follows the lunar cratering record (\protect\hyperlink{cite.neukum2001}{Neukum et al. 2001}): about 10,000 times today's at 4.5 Ga, extrapolated past the record's oldest ages, in the giant impact's debris, 500 at 4.0 Ga and 120 at 3.8 Ga, of slow, yellow-orange fireballs from a main-belt-like population (\protect\hyperlink{cite.strom2015}{Strom et al. 2015}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.marchi2014}{Marchi et al. 2014}), with impact flashes on the closer Moon's dark side; twice today's at 2.7 Ga; and a hundred times today's fireballs at 466 Ma, after the L-chondrite parent body broke up (\protect\hyperlink{cite.schmitz2019}{Schmitz et al. 2019}).

Meteor showers come on their own dates. The modern-era skies have the 38 showers of the IMO's working list, apart from its Antihelion source (\protect\hyperlink{cite.rendtel2027}{Rendtel 2027}, Table 5), with their radiants and drift, speeds, population indices and peak rates, rising and falling about the maximum as \protect\hyperlink{cite.jenniskens1994}{Jenniskens (1994)} measured them, and their outbursts and storms at the hour they were seen: the Leonids of 1799 to 2002, the Andromedids of 1872 and 1885, the Draconids of 1933 and 1946 and others (\protect\hyperlink{cite.jenniskens1995}{Jenniskens 1995} and the IMO's analyses), with the Leonid peaks predicted for 2034; the Leonid storm of 1833 and the Andromedid storms of 1872 and 1885 are among the extended events, at their peaks and from the latitude where the radiant stands highest in the dark. Today's streams are kept for 3,000 years either side of 2000. Showers last thousands of years, so further off, and in the older epochs, the sky has its own, drawn at random with the geometry, speeds and profiles of today's, a new set every 20,000 years, and outbursts at today's rate, about 0.5 a year above ZHR 100 and one in 45 years above 10,000. We take their number to follow the comets that feed them: three quarters from short-period comets, a quarter from long-period ones (only about a fifth of known streams have an identified parent; \protect\hyperlink{cite.ye2022}{Ye \& Jenniskens 2024}), giving about 30 times today's at 4.5 Ga, 5 at 4.0 Ga, today's by 2 Ga and 1.75 at 1.78 Ma, in the comet shower after a star's passage; between the epochs they run geometrically, as the bombardment's decline does. No published work estimates showers in the early Solar System, so these are estimates, and in the bombardment they hardly show against the sporadic rate.


\subsection{Fireballs and impacts}\label{fireballs-and-impacts}

The largest meteoroids do not burn up. Bodies a meter across and more come in as superbolides, brighter than magnitude $-$15, and leave dust trails from about 80 km down to where they break up at 25 to 40 km, which drift and shear in the high winds, as Chelyabinsk's did for over an hour (\protect\hyperlink{cite.borovicka2013}{Borovička et al. 2013}); today about one comes within 1,100 km of a given place every three years, and in the bombardment several an hour. Bodies brighter than about $-$24 reach the ground. When one strikes within 200 km it is drawn with its fireball, a thermal pulse cooling from 6,500 to 2,000 K, the blast wave, a condensation ring and the rising cloud, scaled from nuclear-test and impact-effect relations (\protect\hyperlink{cite.glasstone1977}{Glasstone \& Dolan 1977}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.collins2005}{Collins, Melosh \& Marcus 2005}). Today that happens to a given place about once in 50,000 years; in the bombardment, constantly. In the bombardment epochs, impact flashes also show on the closer Moon's night side, at today's measured rate scaled up (\protect\hyperlink{cite.suggs2014}{Suggs et al. 2014}).

Three impacts on record come on their dates, each a meteor with its energy, speed and path, striking or bursting near the observer: Tunguska, 30 June 1908, about 12 megatons at 15 km/s entering at 35$^\circ$ (\protect\hyperlink{cite.chyba1993}{Chyba, Thomas \& Zahnle 1993}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.longo2007}{Longo 2007}), seen from 65 km as from Vanavara's trading post; Chelyabinsk, 15 February 2013, half a megaton at 19.2 km/s entering at 18$^\circ$ (\protect\hyperlink{cite.popova2013}{Popova et al. 2013}), bursting 30 km up 40 km away; and Chicxulub, 66 Ma, a carbonaceous asteroid (\protect\hyperlink{cite.kyte1998}{Kyte 1998}) 10 km across at 20 km/s, entering steeply, at about 60$^\circ$ from the north-east (\protect\hyperlink{cite.collins2020}{Collins et al. 2020}), seen from 200 km. An airburst brightens down into the thicker air, flares as it bursts, Chelyabinsk to about $-$25, and leaves a glowing cloud that fades by about four magnitudes a second. Chicxulub's hour is assumed, in the evening twilight with the Sun 9$^\circ$ down, so that the asteroid, coming from the north-east, is lit nearly full face against a darkening sky and stays sunlit until it meets the air: by the H, G magnitude system for its size and an albedo of 0.05 it shows as a star some 40 minutes before it strikes, and in its last seconds as a cratered, lumpy disk 1.5$^\circ$ across, drawn with Phobos's map and shape standing in for a face no one knows. In the air its meteor is the glowing shocked air about it, brightening with the air's density from about the Sun's brightness at 100 km to its full light at the ground, where it strikes in a single flash; the fireball, the rising cloud and the condensation dome are drawn to fit their distance rather than at the thousands of kilometers the scalings give. Played faster than real time, the clock slows to real time 30 seconds before each, so the fall is seen at its own pace.

\subsection{Dust, comets and the Ordovician ring}\label{dust-comets-and-the-ordovician-ring}

The zodiacal light, sunlight off interplanetary dust, is part of today's natural night sky. Where the inner Solar System held more dust the excess is drawn along the ecliptic, with the gegenschein opposite the Sun: 30 times today's after the L-chondrite break-up and 50 to 2,000 times during the bombardment (estimates after \protect\hyperlink{cite.nesvorny2010}{Nesvorný et al. 2010}), as bright as a moonlit sky, hiding the fainter stars and meteors. At 466 Ma the speculative debris ring of \protect\hyperlink{cite.tomkins2024}{Tomkins, Martin \& Cawood (2024)} is drawn between 1.55 and 2.75 Earth radii with a normal optical depth of at most 0.00015: lost in the blue by day, a low arch at night cut by the Earth's shadow, and at the equator a source of slow, grazing fireballs from the west. The break-up's dust rises over 50,000 years from 466.1 Ma, holds for two million years and decays with a one-million-year e-folding time, and the meteors with it; the ring, if there was one, decays over tens of millions of years (a 20 Myr e-folding time here). Between the epochs the zodiacal light and the meteor and comet rates run geometrically, as the bombardment declined.

Comets in the modern-era skies are the real ones on their real dates, from JPL's orbits, for the perihelia on record, 1577 to 2127, and Halley's back to its passage of 837 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.yeomans1981}{Yeomans \& Kiang 1981}), its node and perihelion turned 1.22$^\circ$ and 1.04$^\circ$ a century so that the 837 passage comes 0.033 AU from Earth on 10 April (Julian), as recorded, with a tail of some 50$^\circ$ at magnitude $-$3.4. The periodic ones also return every orbital period beyond the perihelia on record, Swift--Tuttle's in 1596 and 1729 and Pons--Brooks's four times from 1599 to 1812. Each of the 24 is listed among the extended events at the apparition where it stood out most, on the day it was brightest against the faintest star that shows at its angle from the Sun, so a comet at perihelion behind the Sun waits for its evenings. Elsewhere comets are drawn at the rate of their time, about one naked-eye comet a year today, 30 times that at 4.5 Ga while the giant planets eroded and then scattered the comet disk, falling to today's level by about 2 Ga, and about half of today's in the quiet Phanerozoic epochs, because the present flux of new long-period comets is still about two to two and a half times the tide-only rate (\protect\hyperlink{cite.kaib2026}{Kaib \& Raymond 2026}). After the $\zeta$ Ophiuchi star's passage the rate swells to about four times today's at 1.78 Ma and dies away over a million years or so, a comet shower. Their heads and tails are dimmed by each epoch's air like the stars.

\section{The sky people made}\label{people}


\subsection{City light}\label{city-light}

In the modern epochs the observer stands in a mid-sized city: a zenith glow of 18.8 mag/arcsec$^{2}$ today (\protect\hyperlink{cite.falchi2016}{Falchi et al. 2016}), 1.6 times today's in polluted air, and held at today's level in 2100 (the recent 9.6\% a year, \protect\hyperlink{cite.kyba2023}{Kyba et al. 2023}, cannot be projected that far). Back in time the glow follows the growth of electric light: only lamplight until 1900, then growing about 3\% a year, an e-folding time of 30 years, between the 2.2\% a year that satellites saw in lit area and radiance in 2012--2016 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.kyba2017}{Kyba et al. 2017}) and the 9.6\% a year in sky brightness that observers reported in 2011--2022. That reaches a third of today's in 1990, near the first World Atlas (\protect\hyperlink{cite.cinzano2001}{Cinzano, Falchi \& Elvidge 2001}), and today's now. Its color goes from gas and incandescent light to orange sodium by 1990 and on to today's LEDs. The glow rises about threefold toward the horizon, and cloud bases over the city glow orange several times brighter than the clear sky (\protect\hyperlink{cite.kyba2011}{Kyba et al. 2011}). Against that glow the city keeps only the brightest stars, and none of the Milky Way.

\subsection{The sky before electric light}\label{the-sky-before-electric-light}

The volcanic year's towns burn oil lamps and candles: Britain then used about one six-thousand-five-hundredth of the artificial light per head that it used in 2000 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.fouquet2006}{Fouquet \& Pearson 2006}), so the town's glow on the sky is some ten thousand times fainter than a modern city's, and the Milky Way stands over the rooftops. The 1900 epoch, the dark nights, keeps that lamplight from the Pleistocene up to 1900, and the city of the later skies is only there once its glow has grown (\emph{City light}).

\subsection{Aircraft}\label{aircraft}

The modern-era skies have airliners and their contrails: about 1.1 aircraft over each 10,000 km$^{2}$ of busy mid-latitude sky by day, from European traffic statistics, at 9 to 12 km along a few airways, quieter after midnight; under half of today's traffic in 1990, twice today's in 2100, none in 1816 or 1900. A contrail forms where the exhaust's mixing line crosses saturation (\protect\hyperlink{cite.schmidt1941}{Schmidt 1941}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.appleman1953}{Appleman 1953}), most often at mid and high latitudes, and lives seconds in dry air but spreads into contrail cirrus lasting hours in the 10 to 20\% of cruise levels that are supersaturated with respect to ice (\protect\hyperlink{cite.gierens1999}{Gierens et al. 1999}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.schumann2005}{Schumann 2005}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.minnis1998}{Minnis et al. 1998}). At night the aircraft show as their navigation lights, beacon and strobes.


\subsection{Satellites}\label{satellites}

From Sputnik on, the skies dated 1957 and later carry the satellites in orbit on their date. \protect\hyperlink{cite.lawler2022}{Lawler, Boley \& Rein (2022)} put every filed megaconstellation on orbit, 65,262 satellites in 30 shells from 328 to 1,200 km, and found the worst naked-eye light pollution near 50$^\circ$ latitude; we let these fill by 2040, and they are the 2100 sky's. Before that each fills as it was launched: Starlink from 2019 (its second generation as it flies today, near 530 km), OneWeb, Kuiper and Guowang, over some 4,000 satellites and spent stages of every other program in the orbits they use, from one in 1957 to 2,200 by 1990. The ISS flies its real orbit, from its element set of October 2026, and its shape grows as it was assembled, from its first module in 1998 to its planned deorbiting at the end of 2030; Mir flies an orbit of its kind from 1986 to 2001. The page has no longitude, only sundial time, so a pass is the one seen at that hour somewhere on the observer's latitude.

Each satellite has a simple shape after its real design, a bus and its solar wings and, on the larger, radiators, modules and a truss, drawn when the zoom resolves it, and its brightness is that shape's. Each face is lit by the Sun and by the sunlit Earth below, each patch of ground by its own Sun's height, so that near the terminator only the lit crescent toward the Sun counts; the cells lie under cover glass and radiators are mirror-like optical solar reflectors, so besides their diffuse light they glint, and mirror the Earth; the wings follow the Sun on their drive, about 2$^\circ$ off where it aims them, and the ISS's on two axes as its rotary joints turn them. Starlinks fly SpaceX's brightness mitigation, their wings edge-on to the Sun over observers in twilight or darkness, with the first generation's buses under visors and the second's under mirror film; so, the second generation comes out about as bright as Lawler, Boley \& Rein's diffuse sphere of 0.8 m$^{2}$, which matched the Starlinks seen in 2020 and 2021, and the first a little fainter. The reflectances are estimates, as no operator publishes its own, and the Sun stands where it does on the date. At 45$^\circ$ an hour after sunset on the March equinox, three satellites are brighter than sixth magnitude in 1965, about twenty in 1990 and about ninety today, a few hundred in 2100; the ISS passes at magnitude $-$1 to $-$2 some 800 km off.

On top of the 2100 fleet are the orbital datacenters modeled by \protect\hyperlink{cite.boley2026}{Boley, Lawler \& Rein (2026)}. Of their three filed designs we take Blue Origin's Sunrise and the relaxed one of their two node spreads: 51,600 Sun-synchronous satellites from 500 to 1,800 km in a dawn--dusk cross of rings whose nodes wander 10$^\circ$ to either side of the terminator. The paper gives each satellite 800 m$^{2}$, panels, radiators and bus together, the size it calls conservative next to designs several times larger, and treats it as a diffuse sphere at albedo 0.2, a deliberately simple reference. We draw a satellite instead, after the designs proposed for such craft (Starcloud's, and Google's Project Suncatcher): two solar wings of 800 m$^{2}$ in all on a drive along the orbit, which follow the Sun but for its share along the orbit, 10--25$^\circ$ with the season; mirror-like radiators; a flat bus. At 45$^\circ$ on the March equinox, an hour after sunset, some 1,600 are brighter than fifth magnitude and 370 brighter than third, less than half as many as the sphere gives, in a band along the terminator, each crossing the dome in minutes; two and a half hours after sunset none is brighter than fifth. On the December solstice an hour after sunset some 4,800 are, until about 9 p.m., and the brightest are glints, the cover glass mirroring the Sun low in the east at dusk, as bright as magnitude $-$4; in June's short nights none is brighter than fifth magnitude.

{\small\itshape before the Moon-forming impact; a guessed $\sim$3 bar CO$_2$/N$_2$ air with impact dust; the young Sun at 17 Myr\par}\vspace{3pt}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/sky_protoearth455.png}

Some 17 million years after the first solids, the gas of the Sun's disk has just cleared, and with it, in the reading of the meteorites' bombardment ages (\protect\hyperlink{cite.edwards2024}{Edwards et al. 2024}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.liu2022}{Liu, Raymond \& Jacobson 2022}), the giant planets went unstable at about this time, some 5 to 20 million years after the first solids; we draw them just before it. Jupiter and Saturn sit in 3:2 resonance near 5.6 and 7.3 AU, and beyond them three ice giants: Uranus and Neptune, nearer than now, and between them and Saturn a fifth giant like Neptune, named here Oceanus, which Jupiter will throw out of the Solar System (\protect\hyperlink{cite.nesvorny2011}{Nesvorný 2011}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.nesvorny2012}{Nesvorný \& Morbidelli 2012}). Neptune still has its own family of regular moons, named here Kymo, Galene, Thetis and Amphitrite; capturing Triton will scatter and destroy them (\protect\hyperlink{cite.rufu2017}{Rufu \& Canup 2017}). There is no Moon: the Earth is still growing, and Theia, the Mars-sized body that will strike it and make the Moon, is a wandering planet near the Earth's orbit, brighter than Venus when it passes close. The Sun is a pre-main-sequence star 17 million years old (\protect\hyperlink{cite.baraffe2015}{Baraffe et al. 2015}): 4,600 K, half today's light, a cooler, oranger disk 7\% larger. Nothing constrains the proto-Earth's air; we take it as 3 bar of CO$_2$ over a bar of nitrogen, with dust from the impacts, so the sky can be seen through it. Where each planet was on any day cannot be known.

{\normalsize\bfseries Early Hadean, $\sim$4.50 Ga\par}
{\small\itshape $\sim$10 Myr after the Moon-forming impact: magma ocean frozen, $\sim$30 bar CO$_2$ left; Moon at $\sim$8 Earth radii\par}\vspace{3pt}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/sky_hadean45.png}
\captionof{figure}{Early Hadean, $\sim$4.50 Ga. Top: noon sky dome at the equator, mid-latitude and polar summer, zenith at top, horizon at bottom, Sun's disk in its own color. Below: sky from the solar horizon to the antisolar horizon with the Sun on the horizon and $4^\circ$ below it.}\label{fig:sky-hadean45}
\end{minipage}\par\vspace{4pt}
About ten million years after the Moon-forming impact. The magma ocean has frozen, the steam of the impact has rained out into the first ocean, and tens of bars of CO$_2$ are left in the air, which we take as 30 bar. A $\sim$30-bar CO$_2$ atmosphere has a Rayleigh optical depth near 5 at 550 nm and about 18 in the violet, so blue light is scattered back to space many times before it reaches the ground. What arrives is a diffuse, shadowless, peach-white glow of $\sim$3,500 K, nearly identical from zenith to horizon, under a young Sun that is only 68\% as bright. The Sun itself is a dim ember at the equator and vanishes entirely at mid and high latitudes: this is a Venus-like sky. Sunset is a brief, deep-orange dimming of the whole dome rather than an event at the horizon. Above it, the Moon is still only about 8 Earth radii away (\protect\hyperlink{cite.korenaga2023}{Korenaga 2023}): 4$^\circ$ across, seven and a half times its width today and 56 times its area, its own magma ocean still open, glowing orange through cracks in its crust, and going round the Earth in 32 hours in the plane of the equator, while the Earth turns in under 6. It crosses the sky in hours, and for weeks around each equinox it eclipses the Sun at every new Moon, somewhere on the Earth, and passes through the Earth's shadow, 14$^\circ$ across at its distance, at every full Moon. Tidal recession will carry it out to about 39 Earth radii by 4.4 Ga. We date the giant planets' instability to 4.48 Ga, the latest that the dated impact record allows (\protect\hyperlink{cite.mojzsis2019}{Mojzsis et al. 2019}), so the fifth giant, Oceanus, and Neptune's first moons are still here; the bombardment ages favor an earlier one (\protect\hyperlink{cite.edwards2024}{Edwards et al. 2024}), as in the 4.55 Ga sky. Through 30 bar of CO$_2$ and the glow of the impact's debris no star shows; the planets and their moons are there to be found by zooming in. The Sun is 67 million years old, near the main sequence: 5,500 K and 68\% of today's light.

Once most of the CO$_2$ has gone into carbonate, Rayleigh scattering falls to roughly twice the modern value. The sky turns pale blue at the zenith but clearly whiter than today (6,900--10,900 K versus 8,300--17,800 K), because the extra scattering fills the dome with multiply-scattered white light. Horizons are milky, and cream toward the poles. The setting Sun is deep red and the twilight zenith, with no ozone yet, is a pale gray rather than blue.

{\small\itshape haze-free, anoxic; CO$_2$ $\sim$0.1 bar, CH$_4$ $\sim$1000 ppm, no ozone\par}\vspace{3pt}

With CO$_2$ down to $\sim$0.1 bar, noon skies are close to modern blue, only slightly whiter. The difference is at dusk. The blue of the modern twilight zenith is produced by ozone absorbing yellow-orange light (the Chappuis band) along the long grazing path; with no free O$_2$ there is no ozone, so the Archean twilight zenith is a pale gray, and the whole sky fades from blue to gray to orange as the Sun sets. Sunsets themselves are much like today's, slightly redder from the extra CO$_2$ and dimmer Sun.

For most of the Neoarchean the sky was clear. Sulfur and carbon isotopes from South Africa and Western Australia, 2.7 to 2.5 Ga, keep returning to the same haze-free background between episodes of haze (\protect\hyperlink{cite.zerkle2012}{Zerkle et al. 2012}; \protect\hyperlink{cite.izon2015}{Izon et al. 2015}): methane at a few hundredths of the CO$_2$, short of the tenth at which an organic haze forms. With no ozone and a twentieth of a bar of CO$_2$ the noon sky is close to a modern blue, a little whiter, and the twilight zenith, with no ozone to color it, turns gray rather than blue as the Sun goes down.

One of the haze episodes the isotope records preserve: three in the 2.65--2.5 Ga Ghaap Group alone (\protect\hyperlink{cite.zerkle2012}{Zerkle et al. 2012}), here at about 2.63 and 2.58 Ga, each lasting about 1.4 million years and thickening over the first 0.3 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.izon2017}{Izon et al. 2017}). Once the biosphere pushed methane past a tenth of the CO$_2$ the haze shielded methane from its own destruction and ran on to a stable $\sim$0.2, where Zerkle et al.'s model holds it: an organic haze at optical depth $\sim$0.15. It pushes the zenith toward a pastel, milk-blue (6,700--8,200 K) and lays a warm cast on the horizon; at the poles, where sunlight crosses the haze obliquely, the horizon turns visibly cream. Twilight is orange all the way to the zenith.

The haze episode of about 2.52 Ga, the youngest in the Ghaap Group and the one \protect\hyperlink{cite.izon2017}{Izon et al. (2017)} resolved, is drawn at the thick end of what models allow. This is the ``Pale Orange Dot'' seen from below. At optical depth $\sim$0.6 the haze absorbs blue and scatters forward, so the entire dome is a warm cream to pale tan ($\sim$5,400 K at the equator, $\sim$4,800 K at the poles) with little zenith-to-horizon gradient, the horizon only a little warmer: the sky looks like a bright overcast made of orange-tinted glass. The Sun is a soft, defined disk (haze scatters strongly forward) at 4,100 K. Sunsets are deep and long: the solar horizon reaches vermilion, the sky 15$^\circ$ above the Sun is amber, and the antisolar sky is peach rather than pink.

{\normalsize\bfseries Paleoproterozoic, $\sim$2.2 Ga\par}
{\small\itshape after the Great Oxidation Event: O$_2$ $\sim$1\% PAL, ozone column 66 DU\par}\vspace{3pt}

After the Great Oxidation Event, about 1\% of today's O$_2$ builds an ozone column of 66 DU, the \protect\hyperlink{cite.cooke2021}{Cooke et al. Twilight is where the thin column shows. With the Sun 4$^\circ$ below the horizon the zenith leaves the gray of an ozone-free dusk ($\sim$6,600 K) and becomes a pale blue ($\sim$8,400 K). It is not yet the deep blue of a 300 DU twilight ($\sim$24,000 K). That saturated blue dusk is in place once the column is near modern, as in the snowball sky at 169 DU. (The pink antisolar arch needs no ozone: it is a Rayleigh effect and shows up faintly even in the Hadean model.)

A frozen planet has dry, clean air and a surface albedo near 0.75, and together they give the bluest mid-latitude sky in Earth's history: 7,900 K at the equatorial zenith, 15,400 K at mid-latitudes and 16,800 K over polar ice. The sunlit ice lights the sky from below and deepens the zenith's blue, 15,400 K against 12,700 K for the same air over open sea, though the horizons whiten much as they do today. Sunsets are golden and comparatively pale; there is little aerosol to redden them. This is the Sturtian glaciation, from about 717 to 661 Ma; the Marinoan, about 639 to 635 Ma, is drawn the same.

{\normalsize\bfseries Ordovician meteor storm, $\sim$466 Ma\par}
{\small\itshape after the L-chondrite parent body broke up: O$_2$ $\sim$17\%, CO$_2$ $\sim$2,500 ppm, a little meteoric dust aloft\par}\vspace{3pt}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/sky_ordovician466.png}

About 466 million years ago the L-chondrite parent body, an asteroid about 150 km across, was shattered in the main belt, and its fragments still make up almost a third of the meteorites that fall today. For more than two million years the fine dust reaching Earth rose a thousandfold or more, and decimeter stones about a hundredfold: fossil meteorites lie scattered through Swedish limestones of that age, and the dust may have helped tip the climate toward the mid-Ordovician ice age (\protect\hyperlink{cite.schmitz2019}{Schmitz et al. 2019}). The meteors here come a hundred times as often as today, most of them slow and yellow with sodium, and we take the zodiacal light as thirty times today's, a glowing band along the ecliptic. The ring is speculation: all 21 known Ordovician craters lie within 30$^\circ$ of the paleo-equator, which led \protect\hyperlink{cite.tomkins2024}{Tomkins, Martin \& Cawood (2024)} to propose that a large fragment broke up inside the Roche limit into a ring that lasted tens of millions of years; it is drawn as a faint, sunlit band that Earth's shadow cuts at night, with its debris falling at the equator as slow fireballs from the west. The air itself is close to today's: about 17\% oxygen, 2,500 ppm CO$_2$, an ozone layer within a few percent of the modern one, under a Sun 4\% fainter, so the day sky is today's blue.

Months after the Chicxulub impact, a global stratospheric layer of soot and sulfate turns the sky into a dim, uniform amber-beige (4,100--4,800 K) with no blue at all, about one-fifth as bright as a clear sky at the equator and 20$\times$ dimmer at high latitudes. The Sun is a pale orange disk at low latitudes and effectively gone near the poles. Sunsets do not happen as color events: the whole dome simply fades to dark brown, and the twilight sky is gray. The soot lets almost no sunlight through for over a year, and has cleared about five years after the impact (\protect\hyperlink{cite.bardeen2017}{Bardeen et al. 2017}); this sky shows for about two.

{\normalsize\bfseries $\zeta$ Ophiuchi supernova, $\sim$1.78 Ma\par}
{\small\itshape clean Early Pleistocene air, under a magnitude $-$11.6 supernova in Scorpius\par}\vspace{3pt}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/sky_modern.png}

About 1.78 million years ago a star of 16 to 18 solar masses exploded in a binary in the Scorpius-Centaurus association. The explosion unbound its companion, which is now the runaway O star $\zeta$ Ophiuchi, and left a neutron star that is now the pulsar PSR B1706-16; tracing both back puts the explosion 1.78 $\pm$ 0.21 Myr ago at 107 $\pm$ 4 pc (\protect\hyperlink{cite.neuhauser2019}{Neuhäuser et al. 2020}). Its iron-60 may be part of the $^{60}$Fe of 1.5 to 3.2 Myr ago found in deep-sea sediments and crusts (\protect\hyperlink{cite.wallner2016}{Wallner et al. 2016}) and on the Moon (\protect\hyperlink{cite.fimiani2016}{Fimiani et al. 2016}). A typical Type II-P supernova (\protect\hyperlink{cite.richardson2014}{Richardson et al. 2014}) at that distance peaks near $-$11.6, about twice as bright as Geminga's, or $-$12.3 if the binary stripped the star and made it a Type Ib. We draw the star where a straight-line trace of $\zeta$ Oph's present motion puts it, near RA 16h10m, Dec $-$21$^\circ$. The stars are moved back along their measured space motions too; Antares has since traveled about 12$^\circ$, and in the sky of the time the supernova sat about 12$^\circ$ west of it. That trace lands at 130 to 140 pc rather than 107, so the position is approximate. From mid-northern latitudes it stays low in the south. The air is clean Early Pleistocene air.

About 342,000 years ago, going by the spin-down age of the pulsar it left behind (\protect\hyperlink{cite.salvati2008}{Salvati \& Sacco 2008}), a star of at most 15 solar masses exploded 90 to 240 pc away, in the direction of Orion (\protect\hyperlink{cite.pellizza2005}{Pellizza et al. 2005}). Type II-P supernovae peak near absolute magnitude $-$16.75 (\protect\hyperlink{cite.richardson2014}{Richardson et al. 2014}), so from the middle of that range, 147 pc, it reached about $-$11: a fifth of the full Moon's light, all from a point. The $-$13 often quoted needs both the near end of the distance range and an unusually bright explosion. It burned in the daytime sky as a dazzling star, cast sharp shadows at night, and held near peak for the three months of its plateau. The air is clean Middle Pleistocene air; at this distance the explosion barely touches the atmosphere (\protect\hyperlink{cite.thomas2016}{Thomas et al. 2016}), so the sky's colors are today's. The star is drawn at the birthplace. The stars around it are moved back along their measured space motions, which puts it just 1.4$^\circ$ from Betelgeuse in the sky of the time.

A Tambora-scale sulfate veil ($\tau\approx$0.4) whitens the noon sky and lights the twilight. Noon skies are milky, a pale blue-white zenith over a white horizon, with a third of a clean sky's contrast between them. Sunsets glow salmon-orange, and the after-sunset sky 15--30$^\circ$ above the horizon turns a luminous pink-lavender: the high sulfate layer is still lit when the lower atmosphere is in shadow. The veil spreads over the months after Tambora's eruption in April 1815, stays through 1816, and clears in 1817. This is the sky of Turner's 1816 paintings and of the Krakatoa twilights of 1883.

\Needspace*{12\baselineskip}
\section{Caveats}\label{caveats}

Paleoatmospheric compositions carry order-of-magnitude uncertainty, and the haze optical properties are taken from Titan-analog tholins; the three haze cases bracket that uncertainty. Aerosol loads for every pre-Cenozoic epoch are educated guesses, and the proto-Earth's air before the last giant impact is unconstrained. The color-matching functions use an analytic fit, and colors are shown without chromatic adaptation, so the tints represent what a modern camera set to daylight balance would record rather than what an adapted observer would ``see'' (an adapted eye would perceive the hazy Archean sky as nearer to white and the modern sky as bluer than shown). Clouds are omitted from the radiative transfer throughout. Orders of scattering past the fourth are spread evenly in all directions, which leaves the hazy skies' zenith up to 20\% bright in twilight, and the blue of the 30-bar Hadean sky is checked against no other code: CalcMySky's had not converged after 60 orders of scattering.

Between the epochs the air follows curves fitted through the epochs' own values and the few dated steps the record gives (Section~\ref{method-time}); the real air wandered between them, and the events' dates and durations carry the uncertainties their sources state, tens of millions of years for the Neoarchean haze episodes. The grid of precomputed airs is set so that blending misses the air between by about 5\% in luminance and 0.002 in chromaticity at most. Beyond the colors, the strengths of the ancient airglow, aurorae, coronae, meteor, shower and comet rates and zodiacal dust are estimates set to show the trend, as each section says; the lunar eclipses, the Moon's orbit and the stars' motions over the last two million years are computed, and the eclipse shadow assumes today's cloud tops in every epoch. Several things are drawn for the eye rather than to scale: the Sun and Moon at four times their size (so eclipses are found and timed with the enlarged disks), the sunset's dispersion up to 24 times, and the limb darkening at a fifth of its strength. Some things cannot be known and are drawn at random: where the planets stood on a given day more than 10,000 years from today, and every star more than about two million years back, where the stars are stand-ins of today's kinds passing the Sun. The Earth's axis turns by the long-term precession of \protect\hyperlink{cite.vondrak2011}{Vondrák, Capitaine \& Wallace (2011)} within 200,000 years of each epoch's year, from today's direction and tilt at that year, since its phase long ago cannot be pinned down. The Milky Way's dark clouds, star clouds and young-star fields before 30 Ma are likewise stand-ins, and its evolution is a smooth model fitted to the Galaxy's history. The scenery and the clouds are drawn, not modeled, and the invented faces of Theia and the molten Moon are illustrations of what such worlds would be like.
